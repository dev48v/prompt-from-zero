PROMPTFROMZERO · DAY 72

🎒 Few-shot budget — every guide tells you to add examples and none of them tells you which ones, how many, or what the last one bought. So this page prices demonstrations instead of recommending them: an 81-input task, an enumerated space of 256 candidate rules, and one mechanical fact — a demonstration deletes every rule that disagrees with it. A query is pinned when every surviving rule labels it the same way, which is a ceiling on what any reader could get right, because when the survivors disagree the answer is not in the prompt at all. The centrepiece needs no parameters: take one record, change one word, and measure. … -> YES deletes 240 of 256 rules; … -> NO deletes 16. Same record, same position, same token count on 81 of 81 records — and across all 81 the number takes exactly two values, not 81, at a ratio of exactly 15.00. 🔴 Then the recommendation that follows from it dies. "Lead with the positives" pins 54.43%, tying the folk rule it was supposed to beat, and on a curated pool it is the second-worst strategy on the board — beaten by taking the cheapest examples — because a YES-only prompt plateaus: six more demonstrations and 104 tokens hold pinned at 13.45% while held-out generalisation falls from 8.95% to 2.63%. A NO-only prompt obeys an exact law instead — it pins k/81, the demonstrations themselves, and 0.00% of everything else, at every k from 1 to 12. 🔴 And the column everyone reports cannot see any of it: the empty prompt scores 92.59% accuracy, a 156-token NO-only prompt scores 92.61%, and the whole accuracy column spans four points while the pinned column spans sixty-six. 🔴 The exact 0/1 knapsack — verified against brute force over all 65,536 subsets — loses to a nine-line greedy, 61.20% against 65.69%, and the classic wrong reconstruction reproduces the DP's optimal value on 59.17% of instances while fitting the budget on 0.00% of them. 🔴 The null result is located rather than claimed: at the default budget the metric you optimise is worth exactly 0.000 points on 100.00% of prompts, at a budget of 800 all ten strategies that put an example in the prompt are bit-identical at 71.775% — and at two slots the same two choices are worth +1.76 and −2.38. 🔴 And the one assertion that failed was mine, not the engine's: a prompt of nothing but NO demonstrations contains no dead example in 2,736 prefixes across both rule spaces, but “NO examples first” is a strategy, and once the pool runs out of NO examples it spills into YES ones — and the pool sizes where it spills are the only ones where it scores at all, 38.69% against 11.88%. The dead tokens are the receipt for the one example that made the prompt worth sending. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

Two things on this page are declared rather than derived, and both are labelled where they are used. The first is the space of rules the reader is entertaining, which is the dropdown above — every result is reported for both settings, and the pair exists precisely because a prompt cannot tell you which one it is being read in. The second is the accuracy column, which resolves an unpinned query by majority vote over the survivors under a uniform prior; it sits beside the pinned fraction rather than instead of it, and the point of the panel it appears in is that it is the weaker number. Everything else — every count, every rate, every ratio — is an exhaustive intersection of bitsets over enumerated sets, so a 0.00% here means zero and not "none seen".

PROBE 1 · ONE RECORD, ONE WORD — exact and parameter-free

No sliders reach this panel. Take a record, write it as a demonstration twice — once ending YES and once ending NO — and count the rules each version deletes from an untouched space. Both spellings are the same length, so the two columns are the same bill. Every one of the 81 records is done, and the interesting thing is how many distinct answers come back.

THE SAME RATIO, DERIVED, FOR NINE SHAPES OF THE WORLD

The 15.00 is not a property of this task. It is ((V+1)/2)A − 1 for A attributes of V values each, and the closed form is checked against a brute-force count over the whole rule space rather than asserted.

THREE WAYS TO WRITE THE SAME DEMONSTRATION

Identical records, identical labels, identical order — different words around them. The evidence is byte-for-byte the same; only the bill moves.

PROBE 2 · THE TWO PLATEAUS — one-sided prompts, k = 1…12

A prompt made only of YES demonstrations, a prompt made only of NO demonstrations, and a prompt that alternates. held is the pinned fraction over the inputs the prompt did not contain, which is the only column that means "generalised". Two of these six rows are exact laws rather than averages, and one of them goes the wrong way.

THE SCOREBOARD AT YOUR BUDGET — eleven ways to fill it

Five rules that cost nothing to follow (free), five that need the version space computed (paid), and the empty prompt as a control. The last two columns are the ones that argue with each other: pinned is what the prompt actually determines, accuracy is what a scoreboard would print.

AGAINST THE EXHAUSTIVE OPTIMUM

Twelve candidates is 4,096 subsets, so the best possible prompt inside the budget can be found by enumeration rather than argued about. gap is points of pinned fraction left on the table; hit is how often the strategy is the optimum.

THE KNAPSACK, AND ITS RECONSTRUCTION

Fitting the most valuable set of variable-length examples into a token budget is a 0/1 knapsack, and it is solved exactly here. Then the table it fills in is asked a second question: does the set that comes back out match the value that went in?

THE BUDGET LADDER — and where every strategy stops mattering

The same eleven rules against eight budgets. Read the bottom row first: it is the whole selection literature's ceiling.

FORMAT AT A MATCHED BUDGET

The same evidence in three renderings, scored against the same token budget. The gap looks enormous.

FORMAT AT A MATCHED SLOT COUNT

Now rescale the budget so the same number of demonstrations fits, and compare all eight strategy rows at once. If a rendering is only a change of units, these have to come back identical — not close.

DO THE TWO METRICS EVER DISAGREE, AND DOES IT MATTER

Two questions that look like one. objective swaps the cheap proxy — rules deleted — for the thing the page actually scores, queries pinned. per-token divides the same score by the demonstration's length. Both are reported as "did they choose differently" and "did the score move", because those are not the same answer.

THE SAME PROMPT, TWO RULE SPACES

Curated pool, so both labels are on hand. Identical demonstrations, identical tokens, identical order. The only thing that changes is whether the reader would also consider a blacklist — which is not written anywhere in the prompt and cannot be.

DEAD TOKENS — examples that deleted nothing

A demonstration that removes zero rules from the space its predecessors already left behind is a demonstration you paid for and did not use. The row with the fewest dead tokens is not the row you want.

THE ONE EXCEPTION — and it is the interesting row on the page

The panel above says a NO demonstration always deletes something, and a census of every prefix of every one-sided prompt in both rule spaces finds no counter-example. But “NO examples first” is a strategy, not a prompt: when the pool runs out of NO examples it carries on with YES ones. That spill is where its dead tokens come from — and, on exactly the same rows, where all of its score comes from. A verifier assertion of mine said this column was always 0.00% and it was the assertion that was wrong.

IN-PAGE ASSERTIONS

running…

"Add a few examples" is a spending decision

Few-shot prompting is the oldest advice in the subject and it is almost always given as a direction rather than a quantity: add examples, add diverse examples, add examples that cover your edge cases. Every one of those instructions costs tokens on every single call, forever, and none of them comes with a number. This page is about the number.

To get one you need a definition of what a demonstration does, and it has to be a mechanical definition or the whole exercise collapses into taste. Here it is the only one available without a model in the room: a demonstration is a constraint. The reader is entertaining some set of rules that could explain the task; a demonstration deletes every rule in that set which disagrees with it. Nothing about language, nothing about attention, nothing about what a model "understands" — just set intersection.

That gives a scoring rule with no free parameters. A query is pinned when every rule still standing labels it the same way. If the survivors disagree, the prompt does not contain the answer — not "the model might get it wrong", but the information is not there, and no reader however capable can recover it. The pinned fraction is therefore a ceiling, and a ceiling is the useful thing to measure, because every real system sits under it and the distance is a property of the model rather than the prompt.

The first measurement is free, exact, and misleading

Take one record. Write it as a demonstration ending YES, and again ending NO. Both spellings are one token, so the two demonstrations are the same length to the character. Count what each deletes. The YES version deletes 240 of the 256 rules; the NO version deletes 16. Do it for all 81 records and the answer takes exactly two values — not 81, not a distribution, two — because a rule fires on a given input only if each of its four conditions is a wildcard or a match, which is 24 of 44, and that is 15.00 to one, forever.

So a YES example is worth fifteen NO examples for the same money. That is a real number and it is the number this page was built to publish, and it is worth almost nothing, which is the actual finding. Rules deleted is not the objective. A prompt made entirely of YES demonstrations narrows the space to the rules that agree about where the concept starts and never eliminates the rule that says "everything is YES", so it can pin the concept's own members and literally nothing else. On the ladder that shows up as a plateau: demonstrations four through nine hold the pinned fraction at exactly the same figure while the held-out fraction falls, because the numerator has stopped moving and the denominator has not.

The other side is worse and much cleaner. A prompt made entirely of NO demonstrations pins exactly the demonstrations it contains and 0.00% of everything else, at every length, on every seed. It is provable in one line — for any unshown input, the rule that fires on that input alone is consistent with every NO you have written, and so is the rule that fires on nothing, and they disagree — and the page prints it as a measurement anyway, because a law you can watch is worth more than a law you are told.

The column that cannot see any of this

Put the empty prompt on the board and it scores 92.59% accuracy. Not 50%, not zero — 92.59%, because the labels are skewed and a reader with no information at all says NO to everything and is nearly always right. A 156-token prompt of NO demonstrations scores 92.61%. That is the entire return on the tokens: two hundredths of a point, while the same prompt pins under one percent of the inputs it did not already contain.

This is not a quirk of the metric, it is what accuracy does on any skewed task, and it is why the scoreboard column is the least informative one on the page: it spans four points across eleven strategies that span sixty-six. If you are tuning a prompt against an accuracy number on a task where one label dominates, the number will move when the prompt changes and it will not tell you whether the prompt taught the reader anything.

The optimiser that is exactly right and still loses

Once demonstrations have different lengths, "which ones fit" is a knapsack, and a knapsack has an exact answer. The page computes it — a dynamic program over integer token costs, cross-checked against brute-force enumeration of all 65,536 subsets — and it loses to a greedy that would fit on a napkin, by four and a half points, and reaches the exhaustive optimum on three quarters of prompts against the greedy's all of them.

The reason is not that the knapsack is wrong. It is that a knapsack requires the value of a set to be the sum of the values of its members, and the value of a set of demonstrations is not: the second YES example deletes almost nothing the first one did not already delete. The objective is submodular, the solver needs it modular, so the only way to use the solver is to lie to it — and then it solves the lie exactly. That is the shape of a whole class of engineering mistakes: an exact optimum of an approximate objective, presented with the confidence of the exactness rather than the confidence of the approximation.

The dynamic program carries a second lesson that has nothing to do with prompts. The number everyone checks is dp[budget], and that number is finished before the reconstruction runs, so it cannot detect a reconstruction bug of any kind. The page implements the classic wrong walk — read the keep-table at full capacity every step instead of decrementing it — and measures the result: it reproduces the optimal value on well over half the instances, and the prompt it hands back fits the budget on none of them.

Everything that is left is a change of units

Terse, key=value and prose are three renderings of identical evidence, and at a matched budget the gap between them is enormous — tens of points, because prose is three times the tokens and three times fewer demonstrations fit. Rescale the budget so the same number of demonstrations fits and the gap vanishes: eight strategy rows come back bit-identical, and at a budget large enough for the whole pool all three renderings land on the same figure to the last digit. A rendering is not a variable. It is a divisor on the budget, and once you have divided, there is nothing else to say about it.

The same is true of the selection rules themselves, and this is where the null result lives. At the default budget, swapping the cheap proxy metric for the objective the page actually scores changes the answer on 0.00% of prompts by 0.000 points. At a budget of 800 every strategy on the board — pool order, cheapest, YES-first, NO-first, alternating, four greedies, the knapsack — is bit-identical, because the budget stops binding and they all take everything. All of the difference between a good rule and a bad one lives in the first few slots, and at two slots the two choices that were worth nothing are worth +1.76 and −2.38 points respectively — one of them negative, so dividing by token cost, which is the obviously correct thing to do, is the wrong thing to do when only two demonstrations fit.

The exception, which was found by a failing assertion

A verifier assertion of mine said the NO-first row always shows 0.00% dead tokens, and it failed on exactly two of twelve settings. The engine was right and the assertion was wrong, and the reason is worth more than the clean version would have been. The census holds: a prompt of nothing but NO demonstrations contains no dead example, in thousands of prefixes in both rule spaces, because the rule that fires on one record and nothing else survives every earlier NO and so there is always something left to delete. But “NO examples first” is a strategy, not a prompt. When the pool holds fewer NO examples than the budget can buy, the strategy carries on with YES ones — and the second of those has nothing left to take.

So the dead tokens appear exactly where the row stops being useless. The pool sizes with a spill pin roughly three times what the dry ones do, and the difference is entirely the handful of YES examples that fell in by accident. A selection rule that never wastes a token is a rule that never buys one either, which is the same finding as the perfectly efficient NO-only prompt one panel earlier, arrived at from the opposite direction — and it only exists on the page because a check disagreed with the code and the code turned out to be the honest one.

And the prompt cannot tell you which world it is in

Every number above is for a reader entertaining whitelists. Add blacklists to the space — the same 256 rules plus their negations — and the parameter-free probe reads 256 against 256, a ratio of exactly 1.00 instead of 15.00, so the entire case for leading with positive examples evaporates. The YES-only prompt, which generalised a little in the first world, becomes bit-identical to the NO-only one: pins its own demonstrations, generalises to 0.00%. And on a curated pool the identical YES-first prompt scores 100.00% accuracy in one space and 68.91% in the other, a thirty-one point swing with nothing in the prompt to distinguish them.

That is the practical form of the whole page. The value of a demonstration is not a property of the demonstration. It is a property of the demonstration and the set of explanations the reader had not yet ruled out — and you can see one of those two things.

What this is not

This is not Day 4's few-shot prompting, which is what the technique is; it is what it costs. It is not Day 58's dynamic few-shot, which retrieves the nearest examples to this query at runtime and has no budget in it — here the demonstrations are chosen once, for all queries, against a fixed token ceiling. It is not Day 65's exemplar ordering, which permutes a set that has already been chosen; here the question is which set, and the order is never varied. It is not Day 71's prompt caching, which prices the same tokens across calls over time rather than against each other inside one call, though the two compose exactly: a demonstration block is the most cacheable thing in any prompt. And there is no model in it — nothing here generates text, scores fluency, or guesses what an assistant would say. It is set intersection, a tokenizer, and a budget.

🔨 How it is built

1. The world is small enough to enumerate completely

Four attributes of three values each is 81 distinct inputs, and a rule is a conjunction over those attributes with wildcards allowed, which is 44 = 256 of them. Both are enumerated once into a bit matrix: for every input, the set of rules that label it YES, packed into eight 32-bit words. Nothing is sampled and nothing is approximated — the version space is an AND of those words, so intersecting a demonstration into it is eight machine operations.

2. A second space exists so the first one cannot be mistaken for the world

The 512-rule space is the same 256 conjunctions plus all 256 of their negations, so a reader in it will also consider "escalate everything except …". It is not a variation for completeness. It is a control: the headline asymmetry between YES and NO demonstrations is a property of the first space and is exactly 1.00 in the second, and every panel is reported for both so that no result on this page can be read as a fact about demonstrations when it is a fact about the rules on the table.

3. Tokens come from text, not from a constant

Each demonstration is rendered into a real string and a hand-written tokenizer is run over it: alphanumeric runs split at six characters, punctuation counts as one, whitespace is free. That is what makes costs vary between examples with the same information in them, which is what makes the budget a knapsack instead of a division. It also makes the YES/NO probe honest — the page checks that the two spellings tokenize identically on all 81 records rather than assuming it, because if they did not, the 15.00 would be contaminated by a price difference.

4. Pinned, not accurate — and both are printed

The primary score is the fraction of the 81 inputs on which every surviving rule agrees, which involves no prior and no model. The accuracy column resolves a disagreement by majority vote over the survivors under a uniform prior, which is the Bayes-optimal reader and the one declared modelling choice on the page. They are side by side deliberately: the argument of the scoreboard panel is that the declared one is the one that cannot see the difference between the strategies.

5. Eleven selectors, five of which are free

Pool order, cheapest-first, YES-first, NO-first and alternate-labels need no computation at all — they are sorts. Four greedies and the knapsack need the version space. The empty prompt is on the board as a control, and it is there because a control that scores 92.59% is the fastest way to discredit a metric. Every selector receives the identical pool and the identical budget, and the greedies stop when nothing left in the pool changes anything, which is why they finish under budget and the sorts do not.

6. The optimum is enumerated, not estimated

For a twelve-candidate pool, every one of the 4,096 subsets is walked with the version space carried incrementally down the recursion and the branch cut the moment the cheapest remaining example does not fit. That gives the best prompt the budget can buy, exactly, so "how much is this heuristic leaving on the table" is a measurement rather than a guess — and the answer for one of them is 0.000 points on 100.00% of prompts, which is not something a sampled comparison could ever state.

7. The knapsack is checked twice, and the second check is the point

The dynamic program's value is cross-checked against brute-force enumeration of all 216 subsets of a reduced pool. Then the reconstruction is checked separately: the returned set is re-scored from scratch and re-weighed from scratch, and both are compared against the table. That separation is deliberate, because dp[budget] is computed before the reconstruction exists and a reconstruction bug is invisible to it — the page ships the broken walk alongside the correct one and measures how often the value check passes while the answer is unusable.

8. Ties are reported as ties

Several comparisons on this page come back exactly equal, and the code tests them at 1e−12 and prints the count rather than rounding them into a small difference. The eleven-way tie at a budget of 800, the 0.000-point metric swap, the bit-identical rows at a matched slot count and the 0.00% held-out law are all of that kind. A page that could not distinguish "very small" from "exactly zero" would have nothing to say in any of those four places.
