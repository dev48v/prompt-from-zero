PROMPTFROMZERO · DAY 83

Prompt ensembling — five different prompts take 70.8% to 87.5%; five rewordings of one prompt take it to 66.7%, for the identical five-times bill.

Step 1 — This is not self-consistency, and the difference is the whole topic

Day 3 built self-consistency: send one prompt k times at a temperature above zero, and take the answer that comes back most often. Every one of those k samples read the identical words, so they differ only by the dice the sampler rolled. Prompt ensembling sends k different prompts — a different phrasing, a different output format, the options listed in a different order — and aggregates their answers. That one change is why an ensemble can beat its own best member and self-consistency cannot: members that read different words can disagree for a reason, and a vote only buys you something when the voters can disagree for a reason. Below, both methods are given the same five-call budget on the same 24 questions.

Step 2 — The members, and where each one is blind

A member here is a prompt variant with a profile: how strongly it is drawn to the true answer, how strongly it falls for each of three traps (an off-by-one, a digit-swap, a rounded figure), and how strongly it is drawn to whatever is listed first. Each of the 24 questions is tempting for one particular trap, so a member goes wrong where its weakness meets a matching question. That is the whole mechanism, and it matters because it is what makes two members fail on different questions rather than the same ones. Read the right-hand column as a blind spot, not a score: terse is the worst member on the sheet and it is still carrying information the other four do not have.

Step 3 — Plurality vote: one member, one vote

The simplest rule, and the one almost everybody means by “ensemble”: count how many members chose each option and take the option with the most votes. It throws away two things — how sure each member was, and everything below each member’s first choice — and it is still enough to move the sheet a long way here. What you are paying for is in the last column: five calls instead of one, every time, for every question, whether or not the members were going to disagree. Hold on to that column; by step 10 it is the only one that decides anything.

Step 4 — Ties are common, and the tie-break is usually an accident

Five members over four options tie more often than people expect, and with an even number of members it is routine. Somebody has to decide, and in most code nobody decided: a loop that keeps a running maximum silently returns whichever tied option the first member happened to vote for, which makes the ensemble’s answer depend on the order you listed the members in. That is not a style complaint — it means the same ensemble, same seed, same questions, returns a different answer if somebody reorders an array. The fix costs nothing: break ties on a canonical property of the options (their listed position, their summed confidence, their Borda score), never on the order of the voters.

Step 5 — Confidence-weighted voting: one member, one vote worth its own certainty

If a member can tell you how sure it is, you can let a sure member count for more than an unsure one. When the members who are right are also the ones who are sure, this is strictly better than counting heads. When they are not, it is strictly worse, and it is worse in the specific way that hurts: a small number of very confident members can outvote a larger number of hesitant ones. Below, three members at around 0.40 confidence are overruled by two at around 0.98 — which is exactly what the rule is for, and exactly what goes wrong when the loud ones are the wrong ones. Nothing in a confidence number makes it a measure of being right.

Step 6 — Borda: aggregate the rankings, not just the winners

Ask each member to rank all the options instead of naming one, give an option ranked r-th out of m a score of (m − 1 − r), and add the scores up. Borda is the only rule here that can elect an option nobody put first, which is precisely the case a plurality vote cannot see: a split field where the real answer is everyone's solid second choice. It is also the only rule here with a real extra cost, and it is not in the call count — a ranked answer is more output tokens per call than a one-word answer, and a member that will not rank reliably cannot be in a Borda ensemble at all.

Step 7 — The finding: an ensemble only pays when the errors are uncorrelated

Here is the same machinery, the same five members and the same 24 questions, with one dial turned: each member’s profile is blended toward the first member’s, so the set slides from five genuinely different prompts to five rewordings of one prompt. Nothing else changes — same seed, same rule, same cost. The gain over the best single member does not merely shrink, it crosses zero and goes negative. This is the number to measure before you ship an ensemble, and the one that is almost never measured: not “are my prompts different?” but “do my prompts fail on different questions?”

Step 8 — A majority of biased members is confidently wrong

The last column of step 7 is the part that should worry you. As the members get more alike, the ensemble does not just stop helping — its wrong answers start arriving with near-unanimous agreement. A diverse ensemble that is wrong is usually wrong 3-to-2 and looks shaky; a correlated ensemble that is wrong is wrong 5-to-0 and looks certain. If you are using the vote margin as a confidence signal, or routing high-agreement answers past human review, correlation does not merely waste the money you spent on extra calls — it actively converts a visible disagreement into an invisible one.

Step 9 — The limit case: five calls of the identical prompt

At temperature zero, sending the same prompt five times returns the same answer five times. It is an obvious statement and it is worth making the measurement anyway, because this is what an ensemble degrades into when the “variants” are politeness edits. Every aggregation rule and every tie-break policy returns exactly the single prompt’s own sheet — there is no rule clever enough to extract a second opinion from a voter who has only one. The bill is still five times. This is also the exact point where self-consistency and ensembling separate: self-consistency turns the temperature up so the five calls can differ, which fixes sampling noise and leaves the prompt’s bias untouched.

Step 10 — The bill, and why k is not a slider you push right

k prompts cost k times as much: k times the input tokens, k times the output tokens, k times the rate-limit budget, and a latency equal to the slowest member unless you fan out in parallel. Against that, the gain is not even monotone — adding a member can lose you accuracy, because a weak member’s votes land somewhere, and an even k turns a large share of your questions into tie-breaks. The honest way to pick k is the table below, run on your own evaluation set, and the honest answer is often 1. Note that the best k here is not the biggest one on every rule.

Step 11 — Which rule, for which failure

There is no rule that wins everywhere, and the ordering between them is not even stable across seeds. Below is the same comparison on seven different seeds of the same engine: plurality beats confidence-weighted on some and loses on others, which is a useful thing to know before quoting a single number from a single run. What is stable here is the shape of each rule’s failure, and that is what you should pick on.

The member: a prompt variant, scored

No model and no network. A member is a profile over the four listed options; a question carries the trap it is tempting for; an error happens where the two meet. The noise term is a hash of (seed, member, question, option) rather than a stream, which is what makes reordering the members provably unable to change what any member answers.

Self-consistency, for the contrast

    

      One member, k samples drawn at temperature T from that member’s own
      distribution, plurality over the samples. As k grows this converges to that
      member’s own argmax — which is the ceiling, and the reason it is a
      different tool from ensembling rather than a cheaper version of it.
