PROMPTFROMZERO · DAY 69

⚖️ Instruction precedence — every prompt engineering guide tells you to put your constraints in priority order, and none of them tells you what precedence is for. It is for deciding which of your instructions gets broken. So this page enumerates the whole answer space — 576 concrete answer shapes across 36 request situations — makes every instruction a real predicate over it, and asks the question first: can this prompt be obeyed at all? At twelve rules, 90.8% of prompts are impossible in at least one situation, and the model gets blamed for it. Then the ranking: re-ordering your rules so the critical ones come last is free and takes critical violations from 7.25% to 2.78%; printing "(priority n)" next to all twelve costs 48 tokens on every call, forever and scores 3.83% — worse than the free fix. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

The first block is exact and parameter-free: whether a set of instructions can be obeyed is a set intersection over an enumerated space, and it does not depend on any number above. The three sliders in the second row are a declared model of which instruction a reader breaks when it cannot keep them all — set all of them to zero and every annotation strategy on this page collapses onto one another, which is asserted below and is the proof that the ranking effect lives there and nowhere else.

THE EXACT LAYER — NO MODEL IN IT AT ALL

Every prompt in the corpus, evaluated in every one of the 36 situations — exhaustive, not sampled. Set intersection is commutative, so none of this depends on the order your rules are in. Feasibility does not care about precedence; precedence only decides what happens after feasibility is gone.

WHERE A CONTRADICTION COMES FROM

A funnel over every pair of rules in every prompt. Two instructions can only fight if they touch the same thing, and only fight in production if their conditions can both be true at once. That last number is a per-pair rate, and it is what makes the per-prompt number a combinatorial one.

Ask the first question first

Here are two instructions from a real system prompt, months apart, written by two people who were both right:

In email replies, keep it to two sentences at most.
When the request is urgent, give at least four sentences.

Neither is wrong. Neither is ambiguous. They are not even about the same thing — one is about a channel, the other about a state. And an urgent email is impossible. Not hard, not ambiguous: impossible. There is no output that satisfies both, so whatever the model returns is a violation of something you wrote down, and the ticket that gets filed says "the model ignores my instructions".

That is the question this page asks before any question about precedence, because it comes first and almost nobody asks it: can this prompt be obeyed at all?

Which is answerable, because the answer space is finite

Make the answer a small object — length, tone, format, language, whether there is code, whether there is a greeting — and there are 576 of them. Make the request a small object too — channel, what was asked for, what language the user wrote in, whether it is urgent — and there are 36. Every instruction is now a predicate: a set of situations it fires in, and a set of the 576 answers it permits. And the set of answers that obeys your whole prompt is just the intersection.

Nothing in that paragraph simulates a language model, and that is the point. It is not a claim about what a model would do. It is a statement about your prompt, and it is exact.

Intersection is commutative. Which means feasibility does not depend on the order of your rules — at all, ever, by construction. Every piece of advice about putting the important thing first, or last, or in the system prompt rather than the user turn, is advice about something else entirely: not whether an instruction can be obeyed, but which one gets broken once one of them has to be.

The number, and why it is a combinatorial number

At twelve rules, 27.6% of request situations admit no valid answer at all, and 90.8% of prompts are impossible in at least one of the 36 — on average in 10.9 of them. Both numbers are properties of this rule catalogue and you should not quote them at your own prompt. What you should take is the shape underneath, because that does travel.

Only 4.47% of rule pairs contradict each other. That is a small, believable, almost comfortable number — the sort of thing you would accept as the cost of a growing prompt. But twelve rules is sixty-six pairs, and 1 − (1 − 0.0447)66 is 95.1%. The measured figure is 90.8%, a little lower because pairs share rules and so their failures cluster rather than being independent. Your prompt does not get contradictory because anyone wrote a bad rule. It gets contradictory because you wrote a twelfth one.

And the conflicts that survive review are specifically the ones that need two unrelated conditions to be true simultaneously. The generator models that directly: it throws away any contradiction whose two conditions are nested — including every pair of unconditional rules — on the grounds that a human reviewer can hold those two lines side by side and see it. What is left needs an urgent email, or a French speaker asking for code. Which is exactly why it does not show up in your evals, and exactly why it reads as flakiness rather than as a contradiction.

Now precedence, and it cannot do what you want

Here is the thing that follows from the definition and is worth saying slowly. If no answer satisfies your instructions, then every strategy for ranking them produces an answer that violates one. Ordering, capitalising, numbering, putting it in the system prompt, saying please — all of them are choices about which instruction dies. None of them is a choice about whether one has to. This page asserts it across every impossible case: no annotation strategy ever reaches zero violations, and the four that only re-arrange words all leave the impossibility rate at exactly the same 27.6%.

There is a smaller version of the same point hiding underneath. Even when your prompt is satisfiable, the instructions that fire never pin down every attribute, so the best answer is a tie — in every one of the 5,400 cases checked, more than one answer scores the maximum. Precedence decides which instruction survives a contradiction. It never decides which answer you get.

So what is precedence worth? Take the corpus and count how often a rule the author marked critical is the one that gets broken. Written in the order it grew in, that is 7.25% of cases. Sort the rules so the critical ones come last — zero extra tokens, one pass of your editor — and it is 2.78%. Print "(priority n)" beside all twelve rules, which is the advice you will find in every guide, and it costs 48 tokens on every call for the life of the product and scores 3.83%. Worse than the free fix.

Do both and you get 2.72%. Which is the honest way to price an annotation: not against doing nothing, but against the free thing you should have done first. 0.069 of a percentage point, for 48 tokens a call. And it is real — the paired difference is positive on all five seeds and lands between 0.037 and 0.102, while the level itself swings a full 0.8 points across those same seeds. The only reason it is measurable at all is that it is a paired comparison; as an absolute number it sits inside the noise of its own corpus.

Both those numbers come out of a declared reader model, and the direction of the result is a property of it: a reader that ignores position entirely cannot be helped by re-ordering. So the page measures the crossover instead of arguing about it. With the default recency weight, printing a number only beats re-ordering once the reader values that number about 3.6× as much as it values coming last. That is a one-afternoon experiment on your own model, and it is a better use of your time than another rewrite.

The only fix that fixes it, and what it costs

If ranking cannot remove an impossibility, editing can. Find the minimal impossible subset, drop its lowest-priority member, repeat until every situation is satisfiable. That works completely — impossibility goes to 0.00% — and your prompt gets 38 tokens shorter. It looks free. It is not, and the column that prices it is one nobody prints: in 20.2% of the situations that were already perfectly satisfiable, the answer now violates something the author wrote. You deleted a rule to fix two situations out of thirty-six, and lost it in the other thirty-four.

Which is what conditions are for. Instead of deleting the losing rule, attach an exception to it — "except in urgent email replies" — so it keeps working everywhere it never conflicted. Same 0.00% impossibility, same 1.27% critical violations, and the collateral damage falls from 20.2% to 5.3%, for about 7 tokens. That is the recommendation this page ends up making, and it comes with its own measured catch: the exception you need is a set of situations, and the exception you can write is a clause. They are not the same object. The clause is usually wider than the conflict, so the rule goes dark in 4.8 situations per prompt where it was doing no harm — and on 0.18 rules per prompt no available clause covers the conflict at all, and there is nothing left to do but delete.

The dumbest thing on the page ties the best thing on the page

Throw away every rule that is not critical. Three rules instead of twelve, 74.5 tokens instead of 222.5, no analysis, no core-finding, no exception clauses. Critical violations: 1.27%. That is not close to the careful fix, it is exactly it — the same 183 cases out of 14,400, to the last one.

Which is not a coincidence and is not luck. Once the critical rules are intact and satisfiable, a critical rule can only break where the critical rules contradict each other, and that is a property of what you marked critical, not of what you did about it. 1.27% is a floor, three separate strategies sit exactly on it, and the cheapest one is a two-minute deletion. If the metric you were optimising was "how often do I break a rule I cared about", you have been buying an answer you could have had for nothing.

The reason to prefer the careful fix is in the next column along, which is why it has to be printed next to the win: obedience to the whole rule set falls from 94.3% to 67.6%, and collateral damage runs to 68.8%. Every style rule you deleted was a rule. A single-column scoreboard would have made the crudest possible edit look like the best idea on this page, and it very nearly is.

Negative instructions build the conflicts you cannot detect

The last result is the one that surprised me, and it is exact. When a prompt is impossible, how big is the smallest impossible subset? Almost always two: 99.1% of the time a pairwise check — the only check that is cheap enough to run on a real prompt, and one you could write this afternoon — finds it. The exhaustive search over triples and quadruples, which costs C(n,3) + C(n,4) intersections and does not scale, buys you the last 0.9%.

But look at what that 0.9% is made of. Every single core of size 3 or more in this corpus — 61 out of 61 over a 1,200-prompt run — consists entirely of negative instructions, all on one attribute, and its size is exactly that attribute's number of possible values. Three "never" rules about tone, when tone has three values. Four about format, when format has four.

Why this has to be true, not just why it was measured

A positive instruction pins an attribute to one value, so two of them collide immediately and pairwise. A negative instruction removes one value and leaves the rest, so any two of them are perfectly satisfiable, and any three, right up until you have removed the last option. A set of "do not" rules is unsatisfiable only when it is complete, and no subset of a complete set is unsatisfiable. That is the definition of invisible to a pairwise linter — and it is why the fourth "never use X" you add is a different kind of risk from the fourth "always do Y".

So the cheap linter is the right tool and the exponential one is not worth building. It has one blind spot, the blind spot is structural rather than statistical, and it has a name you can check for by hand: count your negative instructions per attribute, and if that count reaches the number of things the attribute could be, you have written an impossibility that no pair of your rules can reveal.

🔨 How it is built

1. The answer space is enumerated, not sampled

Six attributes with 6×3×4×2×2×2 = 576 combinations, and four request attributes with 36. Both are built by the same cross product, so nothing anywhere in the engine has to be estimated:

const OUTPUTS    = cross(ATTRS);      // 576 answer shapes
const SITUATIONS = cross(SIT_ATTRS);  // 36 request contexts

An instruction is a guard over the situation plus a req over the answer. 26 requirements × 10 guards gives a 260-rule catalogue, and each entry's satisfying set is precomputed once as an 18-word bitset per situation. Everything else on the page is an AND of those bitsets.

2. Feasibility is a set intersection, so it is order-free

function satSet(active, out){
  out.set(ALL_ONES);
  for (const a of active) bsAnd(out, a.m, out);   // commutative, associative
  return out;
}

The page asserts the consequence directly: shuffle a prompt's rules and the satisfying set comes back bit-identical, for every prompt and every situation. That assertion is what earns the right to say precedence cannot change feasibility — it is checked, not argued.

3. Two core-finders, one cheap and one exhaustive

greedyCore is deletion-based: drop a rule, and if the set is still impossible the rule was never needed. That is n intersections and the result is guaranteed minimal — every member matters — but not necessarily of minimum size. minCoreSize enumerates all pairs, then all triples, then all quadruples, and returns the smallest impossible subset there is. The page measures the gap between them: greedy averages 2.010 and exceeds the true minimum in 0.10% of cases.

4. The reader model is four lines and they are all on sliders

This is the only assumption on the page, so it is written where it can be seen and turned off. Every term needs something the prompt actually says, and every term is bounded — Day 65's rule, which cost that page three attempts:

function weightOf(r, n, c){
  let w = 1;
  if (n > 1) w += c.recency * (r.pos / (n - 1));       // where it sits
  if (r.emph) w += c.emphasis;                          // whether it shouts
  w += c.specificity * Math.min(r.ref.guard.depth,2)/2; // whether its condition matched
  if (r.printPrio) w += c.obeyPriority * (r.prio-1)/4;  // whether a number was printed
  return w;
}

The author's private intent is worth exactly nothing here. It enters the reader's decision only through something written down, which is the whole subject of the page. And the reader picks the argmax over all 576 answers of the total weight it can satisfy — a real optimisation over the real space, not a heuristic.

Because every weight is positive, the argmax necessarily lands inside the satisfying set whenever that set is non-empty. That is asserted (10,436 feasible cases, zero violations) and then used: for a strategy that did not edit the rule list, a feasible situation needs no argmax computed at all, which is what keeps a full render under two seconds.

5. Every strategy is scored against the ORIGINAL rules

Three of the eight strategies change the rule list. If each were scored against its own edited prompt, deleting an instruction would count as obeying it and the crudest edit would win by construction. So the score always comes from the author's rules:

const orig = origActive[s];              // the AUTHOR's rules, active in this situation
for (const a of orig)
  if (bsGet(a.m, out)) sat++; else { broke++; if (a.r.prio >= c.criticalPrio) crit = true; }

This is Day 64's lesson arriving in a new shape. That page found precision made a fallible verifier look free until coverage was printed beside it; here a single "did you break a critical rule" column makes deleting nine rules look like the best idea on the page, until whole-rule-set obedience and collateral damage are printed beside it.

6. The generator's two realism assumptions, both reported

Sampling twelve rules uniformly from 260 produces prompts no human would ship, so two filters run and the page shows what each is worth. One rule per attribute per condition — an author does not write two format rules under the same condition. And the author's eye: any contradiction whose two conditions are nested, which includes every unconditional pair, is discarded on the grounds a reviewer could see it. Turn both off and impossibility goes from 27.2% to 51.1%. The honest reading is that the per-prompt rate is a property of the catalogue; what survives is the per-pair rate and the exponent.

7. The costs are counted, not asserted

A hand-written token estimator (about five characters to a token, punctuation separately) runs over the actual rule text, so " (priority 3)" × 12 rules is a measured 48 tokens rather than a guess. The columns beside every win: tokens per call forever, rules kept out of twelve, answers still permitted out of 576, and collateral damage in situations that were already fine.

8. What the assertions pin down

assert(satSet(shuffled) === satSet(original))            // bit-identical, every prompt
assert(argmax(score) inside SAT whenever SAT non-empty)   // 0 violations in 10,436 cases
assert(infeasible => EVERY strategy breaks something)     // no ranking escapes a contradiction
assert(annotation strategies leave infeasibility EQUAL)   // 27.56% for all four
assert(collateral(re-arranging strategies) === 0)         // exactly, by construction
assert(critBreak(deleteCore) === critBreak(narrowCore)
    === critBreak(criticalOnly) === infeas(critical subset))   // 183/14400, all four
assert(all four dials = 0 => the annotation strategies are IDENTICAL)
assert(every core of size >= 3 is all-negative, one attribute, size === |domain|)
assert(greedyCore is minimal: removing any member restores feasibility)
assert(orderLast costs exactly 0 extra tokens)            // it is a sort, not a rewrite
assert(the maximum is a TIE in 100% of cases)             // so the tie-break is not an edge case

The fifth one is the structural identity that took the headline away from the careful fix: three strategies, one of which is "delete two thirds of your prompt", land on the same 183 cases out of 14,400 — not approximately, exactly. When that assertion holds, the expensive analysis has been proved to buy nothing on that column, and the page has to justify itself on the next one.

9. Three things that were not what I expected

The paid annotation lost, and then it lost again. I built the numbering strategy expecting it to be the answer, with re-ordering as the cheap control. Numbering is worse than the free re-order on its own (3.83% against 2.78%), and on top of the re-order it is worth 0.065 points for 48 tokens a call. The mechanism is legible once measured: a numbered prompt is still in whatever order it grew in, so its printed priorities fight its own recency, while sorting makes the two agree for nothing.

Over-constraint is not the problem — I assumed it was. Twelve rules leave 70.3 of 576 answers standing, and the number of cases pinned to exactly one answer is 0.00%. The story that long system prompts produce robotic output by squeezing the space to a point is not what happens here. Contradiction is what happens.

The best answer is a tie every single time. I wrote the verifier to assert that the engine's argmax matches an independently computed one by index, and it failed once in 5,400. Chasing it turned up something the page had no idea about: the maximum score is shared by more than one answer in 100% of cases — all 5,400 — because the instructions that fire never constrain every attribute, so everything they leave alone is free and equally good. Which means the answer you actually get is decided by something that is not in your prompt at all. The assertion was wrong to ask for index equality; it now asserts score-optimality and counts the tie-breaks, which is the claim the model supports.

And an exact identity broken by floating point. With recency at zero, position cannot matter, so the as-written and re-ordered prompts must give identical answers. They differ on 0.535% of cases. The weights are identical; the rules are summed in a different order; 1 + 1.15 + 1.30 is not bit-identical to 1.30 + 1 + 1.15; and since every maximum is a tie, that last bit is what decides. Set the other three dials to zero as well, making every weight exactly 1 and every sum an integer, and the difference is 0 out of 14,400. The assertion carries a tolerance and the reason rather than a quietly loosened bound — and a real model breaks ties by sampling, so an arbitrary tie-break is the faithful behaviour, not the bug.

10. What this is not

It is not a claim about how any model resolves conflicting instructions; that is the declared part and it is on sliders. It is not the instruction hierarchy between system, developer and user turns — that is a different axis and this page holds it fixed. It is not Day 51's system-prompt placement, Day 16's structured output, or Day 43's constitutional critique, which revises against principles that are assumed to be mutually satisfiable. The exact half — can this prompt be obeyed, what is the smallest subset that cannot be, how does that scale with the number of rules — is a statement about your prompt and holds whatever model reads it.
