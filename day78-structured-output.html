The corpus is invented. There is no public set of model outputs with the correct extraction attached, so all 40 were written by hand: fourteen right, eight well-formed and untrue, eight malformed but repairable, six malformed beyond repair, four more right. Every point estimate would move on a real corpus. What transfers is the arithmetic — validity and correctness are different sets, and the gap between them is countable.

Every output, and what happened to it

Green means the validator accepted it. The column that matters is the one beside it.

1. The validator is answering a different question

A schema constrains shape. It can say that priority is one of four strings and that refund_amount is a non-negative number. It cannot say that this ticket was urgent, or that the refund was £4.99 rather than £49.99. Both of those outputs are perfectly valid, and both are wrong.

Every one of the eight is a value a schema has no opinion about: a plausible email that belongs to somebody else, an invented resolution date, a ticket id in exactly the right format for a ticket that does not exist. Constrained decoding guarantees the format and says nothing about the content — which is what it claims, and not what it gets read as.

2. The gentle repair layer is worth more than it is given credit for

Eight outputs fail the schema for reasons a few lines of coercion handle: a number sent as a string, "High" instead of "high", slashes in a date, one extra key, a currency symbol.

All eight become valid, and all eight become correct. Not a single one is coerced into a valid-but-wrong answer. That is the honest good news on this page: a coercion step applied only where it applies is nearly free.

3. The repair layer that never fails is a trade, in both directions

Once a dashboard is watching the validity rate, the coercion layer grows: an unparseable email becomes unknown@example.com, a priority outside the enum becomes medium, a negative refund becomes its absolute value, a sixth tag is dropped. Each rule is defensible on its own.

🔴 Validity goes to 100%. Correctness genuinely improves, 18 to 29 — those rules are not useless. And the count of outputs that are valid and wrong goes up, from 8 to 11, because six loud failures were converted into six quiet answers and only three of them happened to be right. Both things are true at once, and reporting only the first is what makes it dangerous.

What this measures and what it cannot

The validator, the coercion rules and every count are real code. The corpus is invented, so the exact rates belong to this file. What does not depend on the fixture: validity and correctness are different sets; a validator's precision is a property of the model's failure modes and not of the schema; and any repair that cannot fail has, by construction, no way to tell you it should have.

How it works

The schema is an ordinary support-ticket extraction. The validator implements exactly the subset the schema uses — types, required, enum, pattern, maxItems, minimum, and two formats — written out so the page is not testing a library's opinion of what “valid” means. 

// the whole finding, in two lines
valid:   validate(out).length === 0
correct: deepEqual(out, truth)      // and nothing in the schema knows `truth`

Self-check

Runs on load. Several are controls — a claim that the validator misses wrong values means nothing without showing it catches malformed ones.
