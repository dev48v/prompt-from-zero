PROMPTFROMZERO · DAY 68

📦 Batch prompting — put k independent items in one call so the shared instruction and exemplars are paid once instead of k times (Cheng, Kasai & Yu, 2023). The saving is exact and it is capped: batch size k captures precisely 1 − 1/k of every token batching can ever save, so k=4 already holds 75% of it. What has no cap is the other side. k answers now have to come back in a form that can be put back next to the k questions they belong to — and at k=16 the obvious positional parse hands 22.7% of items somebody else's answer while 43.3% of batches come back corrupted with nothing in the response saying so. Then the control that took the headline: I shipped "count the lines" as the free one-line fix, expecting it to win. It is the most expensive fix on this page. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, offline.

A 2,400-item workload, drawn once and shared by every strategy, so this is a paired comparison: the same questions, the same luck, the same lengths, the same formatting accidents. Nothing here generates text — what is simulated is the token accounting and the channel: how k answers get re-attached to k questions.

THE SAVING IS EXACT, AND IT SATURATES

Per-item input cost is P/k + q, so the saving over one-call-per-item is P(1 − 1/k) out of a maximum of P. The fraction captured is exactly 1 − 1/k and depends on nothing else — not the prompt, not the task, not the model. Everything past k=8 is buying the last few percent.

IN-PAGE SELF-TEST


      


    

  






  

    


      
One prompt, k questions


      
You have 40,000 support tickets to classify. The prompt that does it well is an instruction
      plus four worked examples — call it 180 tokens — and each ticket is another 28. Send them one
      at a time and you pay the 180 forty thousand times. Batch prompting is the observation that
      you do not have to: put sixteen tickets in one call, ask for sixteen answers, and the 180 is
      paid once for all of them.


      
That is the whole technique, and the arithmetic is a single line. Per-item input cost goes
      from P + q to P/k + q. Which means two things worth saying out loud, because
      neither is usually said.


      
The saving is capped twice


      
Cap one is content. The most you can ever save is the share of a
      single-item call that is shared overhead — P/(P+q+a). With a 180-token prompt that is 84%.
      With a one-line instruction it is 47%, and no batch size in the world improves on it. Every
      "batch prompting cut our bill 5×" is really a statement about how bloated the prompt was.


      
Cap two is k, and it is exact. Batching k items captures precisely
      1 − 1/k of that available saving. Not approximately — the page asserts it to
      1e-12. k=2 gets you half. k=4 gets you three quarters. k=8 gets 87.5%, k=64 gets 98.4%, and
      the entire journey from 8 to 64 is worth eleven percent of a saving you already
      mostly had.


      

        
So the decision is not "should I batch" — obviously — but "where do I
        stop", and the token side answers that on its own: the returns die at single-digit k while
        the thing on the other side of the ledger grows without any cap at all.

      


      
The other side of the ledger is not accuracy. It is alignment.


      
Sending k questions is easy. Getting k answers back is a different problem,
      because the answers have to be re-attached to the questions they belong to, and the obvious
      way to do that is by position: line 1 is the answer to item 1.


      
Now suppose the response omits one. Not garbles it — omits it. Every answer after that
      point shifts up by one, and item 5 is scored against item 6's answer, item 6 against item 7's,
      all the way down. One missing line corrupts up to k−1 others. The page's default batch shows
      the shape of it exactly: one dropped answer at item 2, one answer that came back over two
      lines at item 12, ten items scrambled between them — and sixteen lines out
      for sixteen items in, so the count is right.


      
At k=16 this is not an edge case. 22.7% of all items carry another item's
      answer, and 43.3% of batches are affected. The accuracy that a perfect
      channel would deliver is 73.9%; positional parsing delivers 61.5%. Twelve and a half points,
      and not one of them looks like a wrong answer — they look like answers.


      
🔴 The free control that lost


      
The rule in this series is to ship the dumbest thing that could work and let it win if it
      wins. The dumbest thing here is one line: count the lines, and if you did not get k of
      them, run the batch again. No format change, no parser change, free.


      
It works. It takes 61.5% back to 74.0%, which is the perfect-channel
      number to within a tenth. I expected it to be the whole page.


      
It is the most expensive fix here. 92.2 tokens per item, against 45.4 for
      the perfect channel — it slightly more than doubles the cost of the thing you were batching to
      make cheap. And the alternative that beats it is not clever either: print the item's index in
      front of each answer, match on that instead of position, and re-ask only the items
      whose index never came back. Same accuracy — 74.0%, identical to two decimal
      places — at 55.4 tokens per item. Forty percent less.


      

        
The reason is not that tags are more reliable. It is that they are more
        observable. A count check knows that something is missing and cannot know
        what, so its only available repair is to redo everything. A tag makes the damage
        addressable, and the repair becomes proportional to it: 3.4% of items re-asked instead of
        9.3% of items thrown away and 60% of batches re-run. A coarse detector can only trigger a
        coarse repair, and you pay for the repair, not the detection.

      


      
Format and check are two separate decisions


      
Which makes a 2×2, and it is worth reading in this order:


      
    
        
  • positional, unchecked — 61.5% at 45.2 tok/item. The version everybody
        writes first. Cheapest, and 43.3% of its batches are wrong in a way it cannot see.
    • 
        
  • tagged, unchecked — 71.2% at 48.2. Three tokens per answer buys back
        9.7 of the 12.4 points, because there is no cascade left: a missing answer is now just a
        missing answer.
    • 
        
  • positional + count check — 74.0% at 92.2. Full accuracy, double the
        cost.
    • 
        
  • tagged + repair the gaps — 74.0% at 55.4. Same accuracy. 40% cheaper
        than the count check, 22% more than doing nothing.
    • 
      


      
The check matters more than the format — positional-with-a-check beats
      tagged-without-one. But the granularity of the repair matters more than either, and
      that is what the format decides.


      
🔴 The tag is a net negative on small batches


      
I wrote "index your answers, it is never wrong to do" and then had to take it back, because
      the grid says otherwise. Printing an index introduces a failure the positional format does not
      have — the index itself can be wrong — and that rate does not depend on k, while the
      cascade it prevents grows with k. So there is a crossing, and it is measurable: at
      k=4 with a low slip rate, positional parsing misaligns 0.08% of items and tagged
      parsing misaligns 0.25% — three times worse, plus the tokens. By k=16 the tag
      removes 98.7% of the misalignment and the argument is over.


      
Which is the useful form of the rule: the tag is not a hygiene habit, it is a trade against
      the cascade, and the cascade is what scales. Below about six items per call there is barely a
      cascade to trade against.


      
What a count check cannot see


      
It is also not sufficient, and the reason is combinatorial rather than unlucky. The count
      only fails when the number of lines is wrong. A dropped answer removes one; an answer that
      arrives over two lines adds one. One of each in the same batch and the count is
      exactly right and the contents between them are scrambled.


      
That is not a hunch. Every assignment of {clean, dropped, split} to k items is enumerated
      for k up to 7 — 3,279 of them — and 608 come back with exactly k
      lines and a scrambled interior. Every one has at least one drop and at least one
      split, and the smallest case is k=2: split the first answer, drop the second, and two lines
      come back for two items with one of them wrong.


      
The rate is P(≥1 drop) × P(≥1 split), which at k=16 is 3.8% of batches by hand
      and 4.0% measured; at k=32, 26.1% by hand and 26.9% measured. So the cheap
      check does not take the silent corruption to zero — it takes it from 43.3% of batches to
      4.0%, and the last 4.0% still looks exactly like data.


      
The metric that ranks these is the one you were avoiding


      
Tokens per correct answer ranks everything cleanly. At k=16: perfect channel 61.4,
      tagged 67.7, tagged+repair 74.9, positional 73.6, count check 124.6, never batch 269.1.


      
Read that list again, though. Positional at 73.6 looks competitive — cheaper than
      tagged+repair. It is only cheaper because 22.7% of the answers it produced are not answers to
      the questions they are filed under, and the denominator knows that because it was given
      the labels. In production you have the numerator and not the denominator. What you can
      actually observe is the bill and the fact that k lines came back, and by both of those the
      broken version is the best strategy on the page.


      
The label space decides how much of the damage you can see


      
A scrambled answer is another item's label, so it still scores correct whenever the two
      items happened to share one — probability ≈ 1/L. Same 22.7% misalignment, every time:


      
    
        
  • L=2 (is this spam?) — 51.2% of the corruption still scores correct.
        Visible damage: 6.3 points.
    • 
        
  • L=5 — 24.4% masked, 12.4 points.
    • 
        
  • L=50 — 3.5% masked, 17.2 points.
    • 
      


      
Identical bug, 2.7× the visible damage, decided entirely by how many labels the task has.
      Binary classification — the single most common thing anyone batches — is where this bug hides
      best, because half of it cancels.


      
🔴 The safety net that quietly stops batching


      
Re-running a failed batch has a failure mode of its own that took me by surprise in the
      sweep. The chance a batch needs a re-run is 1 − (1−p)k, which climbs fast, and after
      the retries are exhausted the honest thing to do is fall back to one call per item. So:


      
    
        
  • k=16 — 9.3% of items end up in single calls.
    • 
        
  • k=32 — 34.7%.
    • 
        
  • k=64 — 66.7%. Two thirds of the workload is no longer batched at all,
        and it paid for the failed batch first. Cost per correct answer: 342.8 against
        269.1 for never batching in the first place.
    • 
      


      
Its accuracy goes up over that range, which is the tell — 69.1% at k=32 and 69.9%
      at k=64, drifting back toward the single-call number because that is what it has become. A
      strategy whose accuracy improves with batch size is not batching.


      
Latency is not the win, and it is not the loss either


      
Three separate clocks, and batching moves them in different directions. Time until a
      given item's answer exists: 96 ms unbatched, 749 ms at k=16, because your answer is
      behind fifteen others in the same decode. Wall clock for the whole 2,400-item job at
      concurrency 8: 28.9 s unbatched, 25.4 s batched — a 12% improvement while the bill falls 79%.
      And with the count check: 43.5 s, worse than not batching, because re-runs
      are serial.


      
Batch prompting is a token optimisation. Anyone selling it as a latency optimisation is
      quoting the wall clock and hoping you are not the item at position 15.


      
Not the other kind of batching


      
Continuous batching — the inference server packing independent requests into one forward
      pass — shares a word with this and nothing else. That is a scheduler; it improves GPU
      utilisation and changes nobody's token bill, and each request keeps its own prompt and its own
      response. This changes the prompt itself: the items become textually adjacent, one
      response covers all of them, and that adjacency is where both the saving and every problem on
      this page comes from.


      
Where this model is a stand-in, said plainly


      
Declared, not derived: the crowding penalty (a long call answers slightly
      worse — content-dependent, on the call's own input length, and saturating, because the 900th
      token of context is not worse than the 800th); and the format-slip rate, likewise
      content-dependent and saturating, sitting at its floor of 0.002 for a single-item call so that
      the exposure is created by batching rather than assumed for it. Both have a control
      on the MEASURE tab. With crowding off the channel story is unchanged (65.5% positional against
      79.8% perfect); with the slip rate at zero every batch strategy lands on the same number,
      which is the honest way to say the slip rate is an input.


      
Measured, not declared: every token in every table, the 1 − 1/k identity,
      the cascade and its exact extent, the drop-split residual against its closed form, the ≈ 1/L
      masking, the fallback rates, the crossovers, and which strategy wins where.


      
One boundary worth naming: tagged output plus a completeness check reports
      0.0% misalignment, and that is a property of this channel, not of language
      models. The declared channel can drop an answer, split one, or print the wrong index — all
      three break "the tags are exactly 0…k−1", so the check is exhaustive against it by
      construction. A real model can also answer item 7 with item 3's content and label it 7
      correctly, and no structural check will ever catch that. Do not read the zero as a promise.


    

  






  

    


      
🔨 How it is built


      
1. The workload is drawn once, so this is a paired experiment

      
Eight strategies are compared, and the only honest way to do that is to give all eight the
      same questions, the same luck and the same accidents. So the workload holds everything random
      about an item and nothing about how it will be sent:

      
items.push({ truth, wrong, difficulty, luck, qTok, aTok });

// correctness is then a DETERMINISTIC function of the item and the call it was put in,
// so every strategy is looking at the same model
function itemIsCorrect(item, inputTokens, c){
  return (c.skill - item.difficulty - crowd(inputTokens, c) + c.spread * item.luck) > 0;
}

      
Note what crowd reads: the call's input length. Tagged and untagged
      responses differ only on the output side, so the two see a bit-identical set of correct
      answers and any difference between them is the channel and nothing else.


      
2. Two declared terms, both obeying the same rule

      
Day 65 of this series cost three rebuilds to learn that a term which is a constant
      calibrates away exactly, and a term proportional to the signal only rescales it. What survives
      has to depend on content and has to saturate. Both declared
      terms here are written that way and the shape is asserted, not assumed:

      
function crowd(inputTokens, c){          // margin lost to a long call
  const over = Math.max(0, inputTokens - c.crowdFree);
  return c.crowdMax * (1 - Math.exp(-over / c.crowdScale));
}

function pDrop(emitTokens, c){           // per-item chance the response format slips
  const over = Math.max(0, emitTokens - c.dropFree);
  return c.dropFloor + (c.dropCeil - c.dropFloor) * (1 - Math.exp(-over / c.dropScale));
}

      
Both are monotone, bounded and saturating, and both read a length rather than k, which is
      what makes a batch of long answers riskier than a batch of short ones at the same batch size.
      At one item per call pDrop sits at 0.002 — the exposure is manufactured by
      batching, not baked in.


      
3. Four draws per item, always

      
function drawEvents(k, emitTokens, c, rnd){
  for (let i = 0; i < k; i++){
    const a = rnd(), b = rnd(), d = rnd(), e = rnd();   // ALWAYS four, whatever the strategy
    ev.push({ drop: a < p, split: !drop && b < ps, mistag: d < pm, shift: 1 + floor(e * (k-1)) });
  }
}

      
Unconditional draws so the random stream cannot depend on which branch a strategy takes.
      Positional and tagged parsing then see the same accidents on the same batch, and the
      difference in their scores is attributable rather than suggestive. The seed is derived from
      (seed, batch start, attempt, k), so a retry is a genuinely new draw and two
      strategies retrying the same batch get the same second chance.


      
4. The emitter and the two parsers

      
// a dropped item contributes no line; a split contributes two
for (let i = 0; i < k; i++){
  if (ev[i].drop) continue;
  lines.push({ src: i, tag: tagged ? (ev[i].mistag ? (i+ev[i].shift)%k : i) : null, label: answers[i] });
  if (ev[i].split) lines.push({ src: i, tag: ..., label: answers[i], extra: true });
}

const parsePositional = (lines, k) => range(k).map(i => lines[i] || null);   // line i IS item i
const parseTagged = (lines, k) => { ... first line claiming tag t wins ... };

      
The cascade is not modelled — it is what lines[i] does once a line is
      missing. Every misalignment on the page is a consequence of those two functions, which is why
      the demo batch can be read row by row and checked by hand.


      
5. Eight strategies, including the two that cannot lose

      
const STRATEGIES = {
  single:         { k: 1,    tagged: false, check: "count" },   // never batch: the incumbent
  batch:          { k: null, tagged: false, check: "none"  },   // what everyone writes first
  batchCount:     { k: null, tagged: false, check: "count" },   // the free control
  batchTag:       { k: null, tagged: true,  check: "none"  },   // 3 tokens per answer
  batchTagRepair: { k: null, tagged: true,  check: "none", repair: true },   // re-ask the gaps
  batchTagCheck:  { k: null, tagged: true,  check: "tags"  },   // re-run the whole batch
  noChannelLoss:  { k: null, tagged: false, check: "none", perfect: true },  // crowding only
  ceiling:        { k: 1,    tagged: false, check: "none", perfect: true }
};

      
noChannelLoss and ceiling are the two that make the rest readable:
      one isolates the declared crowding penalty from the channel, the other is the best any
      strategy could do. batchTagCheck exists to price repair granularity
      against batchTagRepair while holding the format constant — same tags, same
      detection, and the only difference is whether you redo the batch or the item.


      
6. Repair is the line that only tags can reach

      
if (p === null){
  missing++;
  if (S.repair){                        // we know WHICH item lost its answer, so re-ask that one
    calls++; inTok += c.promptTokens + it.qTok; outTok += it.aTok;
    if (itemIsCorrect(it, c.promptTokens + it.qTok, cc)) correct++;
  }
  continue;
}

      
There is no positional equivalent of this block and that is the whole finding. With a count
      mismatch you have one bit — "wrong" — and a batch-sized repair; with tags you have an index
      and an item-sized one.


      
7. The costs are counted, not asserted

      
inTok  += c.promptTokens + sum(qTok over the batch);   // the prompt, once per CALL
outTok += sum(line.tok for every line ACTUALLY emitted);  // a drop costs nothing, a split costs
// three clocks, because they disagree:
msToAnswer   = when that item's own line finished decoding    // 96 vs 749 at k=16
wallClockS   = total call latency / concurrency               // 28.9 vs 25.4 vs 43.5
tokensPerCorrect = (inTok + outTok) / correct                 // needs the labels you do not have


      
8. What the tests pin

      
// the arithmetic, exactly
assert(savingFraction(k) === 1 - 1/k)                    // to 1e-12, for every k dividing n
assert(savingFraction(k) <  1 - 1/k)                     // STRICTLY, when it does not divide

// the controls
assert(dropCeil = 0  => every batch strategy is bit-identical);   // the slip rate is an INPUT
assert(crowdOff      => noChannelLoss === ceiling exactly);
assert(batch.accuracy <= noChannelLoss.accuracy);                 // a channel can only cost

// the null result, pinned
assert(|batchTagRepair.accuracy - batchCount.accuracy| < 0.01);   // same accuracy
assert(batchTagRepair.tokens < 0.75 * batchCount.tokens);         // at 60% of the cost
assert(batchTag.accuracy > batch.accuracy);                       // positional is dominated

// the honest ones
assert(batchCount.silentBatchRate > 0);         // a count check cannot reach zero
assert(masked ≈ 1/L for L in 2..50);            // the corruption you cannot see
assert(batchCount.tokensPerCorrect(k=64) > single.tokensPerCorrect);  // the net inverts it

      
The pair in the middle is the one that hurt. It asserts that the free control this page was
      built around is matched on accuracy and beaten on cost by something that is not
      cleverer, only better instrumented. Writing a test that pins your own wrong prediction is the
      only way it survives the next edit.


      
And the last one exists because I did not believe the sweep. A strategy whose entire job is
      to make batching safe, costing more per correct answer than not batching, looked like a bug
      until the fallback column explained it: at k=64 two thirds of the work has silently reverted
      to single calls, after paying for the batch that failed.


      
9. Two assertions of mine that were wrong, not the engine

      
The verifier runs 2,026 independently written checks against this engine, and two of them
      caught claims the page was about to make:

      
    
        
  • "A count-clean corruption needs at least three items." It does not. The
        exhaustive enumeration found it at k=2, twice — split the first answer
        and drop the second, or the reverse. A cascade needs somewhere to cascade to, and at k=2
        there is exactly one such place. The assertion now names the number instead of ruling it
        out.
    • 
        
  • "Tagging can only reduce misalignment." False in 3 of 45 grid
        configurations, all at small k, and the mechanism is clean: a wrong index is a failure mode
        positional parsing does not have, and its rate does not depend on k. The invariant is now
        asserted for k ≥ 8, and the exception is asserted separately, as an exception,
        so it cannot quietly disappear in the next edit.
    • 
      

      
Both are written into the UNDERSTAND tab. An assertion that fails is either a bug or a
      finding, and these were findings.
