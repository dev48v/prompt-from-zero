🚫 Logit bias — the one vocabulary-level control an API hands you. It acts on TOKENS. Every rule you actually want to enforce is about STRINGS, and those are not the same object. Nothing here simulates language ability: there is no model and nothing is sampled. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

Two numbers on this page are declared about the world and everything else is a count. alpha is how much more weight a writer puts on a longer piece — a trained model has only ever seen canonical spellings, so it prefers them, and alpha says by how much. esc is the chance at each step that it walks away from the word instead of finishing it. They are the only two, they are both here, and every panel marked parameter-free reads neither. logit_bias is on a dial too, but it is not an assumption — it is the subject: it is the number your code sends, and the centrepiece enumerates the whole grid of it rather than trusting the one under your cursor. The vocabulary is trained once from the corpus and the segmentation graph is exact, so "can this word still be written" is a graph question with a yes or a no and never a probability.

IN-PAGE ASSERTIONS

running…

You are banning tokens. Your rule is about words.

Every mainstream inference API gives you exactly one control over what the model may say at the vocabulary level. It is called logit_bias, it takes a map from token id to a number, and that number is added to the token's logit before the softmax. The documented recipe for banning a word is one line long: run the word through the tokenizer, take the ids, set them all to -100.

The recipe has a hole in it, and the hole is not a bug in anyone's code. A decoder does not emit strings. It emits token ids, one at a time, and the text you read is what those ids concatenate to. The tokenizer's answer for a word is one sequence that concatenates to it. It is not the only one. A vocabulary that contains every byte — which every real one does, because it has to be able to write anything at all — contains at least one other: the word spelled out one character at a time.

The theorem, and why it settles the argument

Line up the two facts. To stop a word being written you must remove every token sequence that spells it. The character-by-character sequence always exists. Therefore any airtight ban contains at least one single-character token.

That is three sentences and it decides the whole design space. There is no surgical ban. Airtightness and collateral damage are not two properties you can trade off against each other with a cleverer choice of tokens — they are the same knob. If your ban is precise it is leaky, and if it is airtight it has taken a letter out of the alphabet.

The page checks it rather than asserting it: every strategy, every word, and for each of the airtight ones, whether a single-character token is in the set. Sixty-two of sixty-two, no violations. And the converse is checked too, because it would be convenient and it is false: five cells ban a character and the word survives anyway, since one banned letter can be covered by a longer piece somewhere else in the word.

What the documented ban actually does

Across sixteen words it leaves thirteen writable. That is the number I did not expect: I built this to show it leaks on everything, and it does not. Three words are genuinely stopped.

Look at which three. They are the words whose canonical encoding already contained single characters — the words the tokenizer had failed to merge into pieces, so encode() handed back letters and the ban swept them up by accident. The three it stopped cost 30.33 ordinary words each; the thirteen it missed cost 5.08. The ban is expensive exactly where it is effective, and cheap exactly where it does nothing. There is no configuration in which it is both.

Which is why the vocabulary ladder is the table to sit with. Same words, same corpus, same code; only the merge list is truncated. At 135 tokens the vocabulary spells these words out in six pieces on average and the ban holds every one of them. At 495 tokens it spells them in 2.63 pieces and the ban holds three. The guardrail was never doing the work. The tokenizer's ignorance was.

The dial is a discount

-100 is not infinity and the API clamp is not the interesting part. Adding a finite number to a logit multiplies a weight by e^bias; the token stays in the distribution, at e^-100 of its former weight, and if it is the only way to finish something the model wants to finish, it is still the most likely thing on the list. On this grid the word keeps a strictly positive probability on 191 of 192 cells, and the one zero is a float64 underflow — the shortest spelling of that word needs seven banned tokens, so the weight is e^-1400 and the hardware runs out before the model does.

There is a second consequence, and it is the one that surprised me. Because a softmax renormalises, the weight a bias takes off one spelling is not deleted — it is handed to the word's other spellings. So the probability of the banned word is not even monotone in the bias: six of the 1,232 adjacent steps down the grid raise it. The effect is small here, at most 3.02e-5, and it cannot be designed away, because it is what renormalisation is. On ␣delete the whole ban is worth 0.43% of the word's probability.

The other half of the dial argument is a genuine null. From -50 to -200 the emitted probability is bit-identical, and from -20 it agrees to six decimals. Everything that a large negative bias can buy has already been bought by a small one. Whether your provider clamps at -100 makes no difference to any number on this page.

Three repairs, in the order I reached for them

Ban every surface form. The obvious one, and the one most codebases end up with. It cuts the leak from thirteen words to five — and it bans 12.44 tokens per word instead of 2.63, touching 53.88 of the 381 ordinary words. It still misses the loudest spelling of all: the ALL-CAPS form is untouched on sixteen of sixteen, because upper-case text is tokenized by an almost disjoint set of pieces.

Compute the smallest airtight ban. This is the one that feels like engineering, and it optimises the wrong number. Minimising the size of the ban and minimising its damage give different sets: 21.13 words touched against 8.63, for the same airtightness, at a cost of a quarter of a token more per word. The reason is mechanical — the smallest cut is usually a single very common letter, and a common letter is common in the rest of the language too. At blocklist scale the inversion is plainer: the 29-token blocklist damages more of the lexicon than the 37-token one.

Ban every token that could ever appear in the word. Airtight, obviously, and it takes out 23.49% of the corpus per word. This is the option that ends with someone filing a bug saying the model has started spelling things strangely.

The control that wins is not a control at all

Decode the reply and look for the string. It bans nothing, so it damages nothing. It cannot leak, because it tests the object your rule is about rather than a proxy for it. It handles capitals, plurals, leading spaces and the character-by-character spelling for free, because all of those are the same string test. Its entire price is that a rejected reply has to be asked for again: 1/(1-q) calls, which is 1.111 if one reply in ten would have contained the word and 1.010 if one in a hundred would.

That is the shape of almost every result on this page. The token-level control is a proxy for a string-level rule, the proxy is not equivalent to the rule, and the gap is not a tuning problem. logit_bias is genuinely useful for what it actually is — nudging a choice between two known tokens, forcing a classification into a closed set of single-token labels, discouraging a formatting habit — and every one of those works because the thing being controlled is a token in the first place.

This is not Day 76. That page was about the order in which temperature and the truncators are applied to a distribution; this one changes no sampler and asks what happens when you edit the vocabulary the distribution is over. It is not Day 74 either: that page was about where the PROMPT is cut and whether its last token is a boundary of what follows. This is the output side, and the question is not which spelling is likely but which spellings remain possible.

🔨 How it is built

Every number on the MEASURE tab comes from the block between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END in this file's source. It touches no DOM, reads no clock and calls no random number generator. An independent Node verifier rebuilds each load-bearing structure a second way — BPE retrained with pair counts recomputed from scratch every round, encoding through a character trie, segmentations enumerated explicitly instead of counted by dynamic programming, path totals in BigInt, cuts re-derived by exhaustive subset search, and the probability re-summed path by path — and compares them cell for cell.

1. The vocabulary is trained, not written

A hand-picked vocabulary would be a way of rigging the result, so there isn't one. A 6,497 character corpus of ordinary support-desk prose is DATA; a real BPE merge loop counts adjacent symbol pairs across the word table, merges the commonest, and repeats 420 times, with ties broken by first appearance so the training is deterministic. The base alphabet is declared rather than derived — 75 characters, upper and lower case, digits and punctuation — because a byte-level vocabulary contains every byte whether the corpus used it or not, and that single property is what the theorem rests on.

Words are pre-tokenized the way GPT-2 does it: a leading space belongs to the word after it, and a run of letters, a run of digits and a run of punctuation are separate pieces. So " card" and "card" are different strings and become different tokens, which is why the leading-space variant of a word is a real problem and not a curiosity.

2. Encoding is the real algorithm, not the easy one

Canonical encoding repeatedly merges the surviving pair with the lowest merge rank. Applying the merge list in order, one full pass per merge, is a different function — a later merge can create an opportunity for an earlier one — and using it would have quietly produced encodings no tokenizer returns. The verifier re-implements the same semantics over a linked structure with a candidate set instead of a linear rescan.

3. A word becomes a graph, and the ban becomes a cut

Node i is "i characters written"; an edge from i to j is a vocabulary token equal to s[i..j). A path from 0 to the end is a token sequence that produces the word, so "can the model still write it" is reachability, "how many ways" is a path count, "how much clumsier" is a shortest path, and "what does an airtight ban look like" is a minimum hitting set over token types. None of that is estimated.

4. Cuts are found exactly, by branching on a path

A cut must hit every path, so it must hit any particular path you can show it. That gives an exact search: find one surviving spelling, branch on each distinct token in it, recurse, and iterate the depth limit upward until something works. It terminates against a guaranteed upper bound — banning every token that can start the word is always a cut, because no path can skip the first node. The verifier does not reuse any of this: it enumerates every subset of the word's usable tokens up to the claimed size and checks that none smaller is a cut.

5. Damage is measured on the rest of the language

The lexicon is every distinct word of the corpus with its leading space, 381 of them, each with its canonical encoding and its shortest spelling. A ban makes a word dead if no path survives and respelled if its canonical encoding is hit but something else gets through — which is not a harmless outcome, since the replacement is longer and is a spelling the model has never produced. Words the ban cannot reach at all — no token of their segmentation graph is in it — are asserted bit-identical afterwards, which is what makes the counted ones meaningful. Note that the weaker version of that test is wrong and the assertions caught it: BPE's own encoding is not always the shortest spelling, on 3 of the 381 words, so "the ban did not touch its encoding" and "nothing about the word changed" are different statements.

6. The declared half is a forward pass, not a formula

At each node the writer chooses among the tokens that continue the word, weighted by e^(alpha · (len-1)), against one competing weight that represents everything else in the vocabulary and is solved for so that the escape probability is exactly esc before any ban. The bias is added to the logit of banned tokens, exactly as an API would. The probability of the word is then a forward pass over the graph — an exact sum over every spelling, not a sample and not an approximation of one. Setting the bias on the competing weight as well makes the whole thing a no-op, which is the softmax shift-invariance control.

7. The claims are attacked rather than restated

The in-page assertions check that a ban can never increase the number of spellings or lower the token count; that a word the ban does not touch is unchanged to the last bit, across every lexicon word and every strategy; that reachability and a positive path count are the same predicate; that every airtight ban contains a single-character token; that the character path decodes to the word it came from; that the claimed minimum cut is a cut and that no smaller subset of the word's usable tokens is; that a bigger ban is never weaker than the smaller one it contains; that probability is monotone in the bias and stays inside [0,1]; that a zero bias is identical to no ban at all; that the vocabularies on the ladder are genuinely nested; and that the cheapest airtight ban never costs less than the floor the theorem gives it.

8. The controls are printed, not described

Biasing the entire vocabulary equally must change nothing, a bias of -700 must equal deleting the token, a bias applied to an empty set must be the identity, banning every usable token must leave exactly zero spellings, and the character path must decode back to its word. All five are swept over the whole declared grid — ten alphas by five escape rates by sixteen words — rather than checked at the dials, because a control that only holds where you happen to be standing is not a control.

9. Nothing expensive runs twice

The parameter-free sweeps depend on the corpus and nothing else, so they are computed once and reused; the panels that read a dial are recomputed on change, in a plain timer-driven pump so a slider drag never blocks the first paint. There is no animation frame, no clock and no random source anywhere in the file.
