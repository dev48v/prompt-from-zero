PROMPTFROMZERO · DAY 71

💾 Prompt caching — everyone quotes the discount (a cache read is a tenth of the price) and nobody quotes the surcharge, the floor, the window, or the fact that the one-line version of the feature is a pure tax on one of the two most common workload shapes. So this page builds the mechanism instead of describing it: a byte-prefix matcher over an ordered block list, a TTL clock, the published price schedule and the four published minimum-prefix tiers, run forward over deterministic traces. The centrepiece needs no parameters at all — take one 14-token line, Current date: …, which is 0.22% of a 6,324-token prompt, and move it through the eight slots of that prompt in turn. The bill takes two values, not eight: slots 0–5 are bit-identical at 1.245× with a 0.00% hit rate, slots 6–7 are bit-identical at 0.157×. Note the direction of the first number — above 1.000, so caching a badly-ordered prompt costs more than not caching it. 🔴 Then the traps, each of which returns HTTP 200: top-level auto-caching on a shared-prefix workload scores 0.00% and bills 1.250×; past the TTL the bill is 1.245× forever at a 0.00% hit rate while under the minimum prefix the same 0.00% costs exactly 1.000× — two silent zeros with opposite bills; an agent turn appending 21 blocks falls off the lookback window and re-reads the whole conversation at full price, which one extra array entry and zero tokens fixes; and 32 concurrent identical prefixes pay 8.173× the staggered bill. 🔴 The claim I built the page to make died: four breakpoints spread "to be safe" do not tie two well-placed ones, they lose to one — 0.357× against 0.155×. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

Only three numbers on this page are declared rather than derived, and all three are published constants of the feature rather than guesses about a model: the read multiplier 0.10, the write multipliers 1.25 (5 minute) and 2.00 (1 hour), and the block lookback window, whose exact off-by-one is a reading — the existence and position of the cliff it causes are not. The four minimum-prefix tiers in the dropdown are the four published ones. Everything else — every hit rate, every bill, every ratio — is the prefix matcher run forward over a trace, so a 0.00% here means zero and not "none seen".

PROBE 1 · ONE LINE, EIGHT SLOTS — exact and parameter-free

The same prompt every time. The same breakpoint every time. The only thing that moves is where a 14-token Current date: line sits. Two placements are shown: the one a person writes once by hand and never revisits (fixed — cache_control attached to a specific block), and the one three lines of code can do (adaptive — put it on the last block that did not change since the previous call).

PROBE 2 · THE AUDIT CHECKLIST, ENUMERATED — all 16 subsets

Four defects every caching guide lists, in the same breath, as things to remove from your prefix. Every subset of them is run, so the question "what is fixing this one worth?" is answered for all sixteen states rather than asserted for one.

AND THE FOURTH DEFECT IS NOT LIKE THE OTHER THREE

A conditional system section does not zero your cache. It divides it, into 2F separate entries, each of which has to be written before it can be read.

THE FLOOR — four published minimums, one prompt

Below the minimum, nothing is cached and nothing says so: cache_creation_input_tokens comes back 0 and the request succeeds. The tiers are not monotonic across model generations.

FIVE BREAKPOINT SCHEMES, TWO WORKLOADS, TWO ORDERINGS

The costs nobody prints beside the discount: what the scheme wrote, and what it billed against writing nothing at all. A ratio above 1.000 means turning caching on made this workload more expensive.

BREAK-EVEN — how many reads pay for one write

Exact, and it is not a modelling choice: the write costs m× and every later read costs 0.1×, so the crossing is where m + 0.1(n−1) = n.

AND WHAT HAPPENS WHEN THE CALLS ARE FURTHER APART THAN THE TTL

Every entry has expired before the next call arrives, so every call is a write and no call is ever a read. The hit rate is not low. It is zero, and the bill is not neutral.

THE LOOKBACK CLIFF — and the breakpoint that costs nothing

A breakpoint searches backwards a bounded number of content blocks for something to extend. An agentic turn that appends more blocks than that fits nowhere in the window, so the whole conversation is re-read at full price and the request still succeeds. The fix is a second breakpoint on the first new block: zero extra tokens, one array entry.

FAN-OUT — N identical prefixes at once

An entry is readable only once the first response has begun. Fire N together and none of them can read what the others are still writing.

WHAT SURVIVES WHAT — derived, not restated

Change exactly one thing on call k and measure what call k could still read. The published tier table falls straight out of "a prefix is a prefix".

THE MULTI-TENANT BLIND SPOT

Two defects on the same checklist behave completely differently: a per-request invalidator costs you a rate, and a per-user one costs you a constant — one extra write per user, forever invisible to a benchmark with one user in it. Sweeping calls-per-user with 40 users in the trace.

THE MID-CONVERSATION INSTRUCTION, PRICED

Turn 8 of a 15-turn session needs a new operator instruction. You can append it to the top-level system prompt, which sits in front of everything, or send it as a message after the history. Same words, same tokens, different position.

The discount is the boring half

Prompt caching is sold as a discount, and the discount is real: a token served from cache costs a tenth of a token processed fresh. Every page that explains the feature leads with that number, shows where to put cache_control, and stops. What none of them price is the other side of the ledger, which has four entries — a write surcharge you pay whether or not anyone ever reads the entry, a minimum length below which nothing is cached and nothing is reported, a bounded backwards search that fails silently on exactly the workload people build agents out of, and a visibility delay that makes concurrency the worst case rather than the best one.

All four have the same shape: the request succeeds. There is no error, no warning, no exception. The only symptom is a number in usage that nobody looks at, and a bill that is somewhere between slightly and dramatically higher than the one you budgeted for. That is what this page measures — not "does caching save money", which is a settled question, but "what does the thing cost when it is quietly not working", which is not.

There is exactly one invariant, and everything is downstream of it

A cache key is the bytes of the prompt up to a breakpoint. Not a summary of them, not a semantic fingerprint — the bytes. Change one character at position n and every breakpoint at or after n misses. That is the whole mechanism, and the reason it is worth building rather than describing is that the consequences are counter-intuitive in both directions.

Downward: the cost of a stray token is not proportional to the token. A 14-character date stamp and a 2,600-token system prompt do exactly the same damage if they sit in the same place, because what you lose is not the changed thing, it is everything after it. Probe 1 makes that literal — the same 14 tokens are moved through eight slots and the bill takes two values, not eight.

Upward: the damage is bounded by position in a way that rewards a rule nobody writes. A breakpoint placed by hand sits on one specific block forever, so a volatile line inserted anywhere in front of it destroys the entire prefix. A breakpoint placed by a three-line rule — put it on the last block that is byte-identical to last call's — keeps whatever prefix survives, so the volatile line only costs you the part of the prompt that is behind it. The two columns of Probe 1 are the same prompt, the same trace, the same volatile line, and the difference between them is entirely the placement rule.

The one-line version of the feature has a workload it is wrong for

Top-level automatic caching puts the breakpoint on the last cacheable block, which is the simplest possible instruction and is correct for a conversation: this turn's last block is next turn's history, so the entry you wrote is exactly the entry you want to read. Run the same line of code against the other extremely common shape — a large shared preamble with a different question stapled to the end of it, which is every classifier, every extraction job, every fan-out — and the breakpoint lands after the question. The key is unique on every call. It is written every call, read never.

That does not cost you the benefit. It costs you the surcharge and the benefit: the bill is strictly above the bill for not caching at all, and the hit rate is exactly zero. The scheme table has both workloads and the same five schemes in each, and the interesting column is the last one, where a ratio above 1.000 means enabling the feature made this workload more expensive than leaving it off.

Two silent zeros with opposite bills

A prefix shorter than the model's minimum is not cached. A prefix whose calls are further apart than the TTL is cached and always expired. Both report a hit rate of zero, and they are not the same failure at all. Below the floor there is no entry, so there is no write, so the bill is exactly the uncached bill — the feature does nothing, for nothing, and the only cost is the hour you spend wondering why. Beyond the TTL the entry is written, every single call, and read on none of them, so the bill is the write multiplier applied to your entire prefix, forever. One is a no-op and one is a standing 25% surcharge, and cache_read_input_tokens === 0 is the same in both.

The floor has a second edge worth knowing about: the four published minimums are 512, 1024, 2048 and 4096 tokens, and they are not ordered by model generation. The same prompt, the same code, the same breakpoint caches on one model and silently does not on another, and the direction is not the one you would guess.

The checklist mixes two categories that measure differently

Every guide's audit list runs the same way: no timestamps in the system prompt, no request IDs, sort your JSON keys, don't build the tool list per user, watch conditional sections. Enumerating all sixteen subsets of four of those makes two things visible that the list itself hides.

First, the per-request ones are absorbing. Any one of them alive takes the hit rate to zero, so fixing three of the three-and-a-bit is worth exactly nothing. The marginal value of the second fix is 0.00, the third is 0.00, and the last one is worth all of it. That is a genuinely awkward property for a checklist to have, because a checklist implies partial credit and there is none.

Second, one of the four is not in that category at all. A conditional system section does not make the prefix unique per call — it makes it one of 2F prefixes, each of which caches perfectly well once written. So the damage is not a rate, it is a division, and the size of the section is irrelevant: a 95-token if block and a 2,600-token one halve your cache identically. It only becomes catastrophic when 2F approaches the number of calls you make inside one TTL, at which point every call writes a fresh entry and you are back to the standing surcharge.

And the per-user defects are worse than either, in a way that is specifically undetectable: putting the user's name in the system prompt costs one extra write per user. On a benchmark with one user, that is one write — invisible. On a trace with forty users and a few calls each, it is the whole bill. The tenant sweep holds everything else fixed and moves only calls-per-user, and the same defect goes from unmeasurable to dominant without changing a line of the prompt.

The cliff that has nothing to do with your prompt

A breakpoint does not search the whole history for something to extend; it searches a bounded number of blocks backwards. In a chat that is irrelevant, because a turn is a block or two. In an agent it is not, because one turn can be twenty tool calls and twenty tool results, and the moment a turn appends more blocks than the window is wide, the breakpoint looks back, finds nothing it recognises, and the entire accumulated context is re-read at full price. The loop still works. The answers are still right. The bill steps.

The fix costs nothing at all: put a second breakpoint on the first block of the new turn. It is one block away from the entry you want, so it always lands inside the window, and the read it recovers extends all the way back through the history. No extra tokens, no extra instruction, one more entry in an array — and the cliff is gone for any turn size, because the read comes from the low breakpoint and the write goes to the high one.

Ordering beats placement, and it is free

Given a prompt in the wrong order, no breakpoint scheme rescues it. Given a prompt in the right order, the cheapest scheme captures nearly all of the available saving and the expensive ones add very little. That ranking is stable across both workloads and both TTLs, and it is the practical summary of the whole page: the first-order lever is which segment goes where, which is a text edit and costs zero tokens; the second-order lever is where the breakpoints go, which is also free but only pays once the ordering is right; and the third-order lever — how many breakpoints, which TTL — is where all the documentation is.

What this is not

This is not Day 52's context management, which decides what to keep in the window; caching decides what you pay for what you already decided to keep, and the two can disagree — trimming an old tool result shortens your prompt and invalidates your prefix, so it can raise the bill. It is not Day 68's batch prompting, which pays a shared preamble once by putting k items in one call; caching pays it once across calls over time, and the two compose. It is not Day 41's streaming, which is about when tokens arrive — though the visibility rule in the fan-out panel is the one place the two touch. And there is no model in it: nothing here simulates language ability, generates text, or guesses what an assistant would say. It is a prefix matcher, a clock, and a price list.

🔨 How it is built

1. A prompt is an ordered list of blocks, and that is all it is

Every part of the prompt — the tool definitions, the system prompt, a retrieved document, one turn of history, this call's question — becomes a run of content blocks carrying a token count and a signature. The signature is the part's identity plus whatever makes it vary: nothing at all for a frozen system prompt, the user id for a persona, the call index for a date stamp. Two prompts share a cache prefix exactly when their signature lists agree block for block from the start, which is the byte-prefix rule stated in the only terms the mechanism actually needs.

2. The key chain is incremental, and it is checked against the naive one

Prefix keys are a two-lane FNV-1a chain so a 200-block prompt costs 200 hash steps rather than 200 string concatenations. That is an optimisation, and optimisations are where engines quietly become wrong, so the page also builds the obvious version — literally joining every signature up to i — and asserts that the two agree on which prefixes are equal, for every pair of blocks in several generated prompts. The fast chain is never trusted on its own.

3. Serving a call is four lines of arithmetic

Sort the breakpoints, refuse more than four. For each, walk backwards up to the window looking for a live entry; keep the longest hit found by any of them. Every breakpoint whose cumulative prefix meets the minimum writes an entry, and the tokens written are the span from the end of the read to the last such breakpoint. What remains is uncached. Then bill = read×0.10 + written×m + uncached×1.00, which is the published schedule and nothing else. There is no fitted parameter anywhere in that expression.

4. Two breakpoint placers, because the difference between them is a result

fixed attaches the breakpoint to a named block and leaves it there, which is what a person does once and never revisits. adaptive recomputes it every call as the last block that did not change, which is three lines and which nobody writes. Both are run over the identical trace. Probe 1 exists to show that on the same prompt with the same defect, one of them takes a 25% standing surcharge and the other takes none.

5. The producer is a trace, not a distribution

There is no sampling in any outcome column. A trace is a deterministic sequence of calls with a clock, the store is a real map with real expiry, and the numbers are what the matcher did. The only randomness on the page is which user index a tenant-trace call belongs to, and that is seeded. This is why a rate of 0.00% can be printed without hedging: it is not an estimate that came out low.

6. Subsets are enumerated rather than argued

Four defects is sixteen states, so all sixteen are run. That is the difference between "removing a timestamp is important" and the actual finding, which is that fourteen of the sixteen rows are bit-identical and the checklist has no partial credit in it. The same approach drives the flag sweep (2F prefixes for F = 0…6), the floor (11 prefix sizes × 4 published minimums), and the cliff (every turn size from 1 to 40).

7. The invalidation hierarchy is derived, not typed in

The published table says a tool change invalidates everything, a system change spares the tools, a message change spares both. That table is never entered into this page. Each mutation is applied to one call in a trace and the read tokens on the next call are measured, and the hierarchy comes out because a prefix is a prefix. If the mechanism were wrong, the derived table would disagree with the published one, which is a check rather than a restatement.

8. What is declared, and where it is on the page

Three constants: the read multiplier, the two write multipliers, and the lookback window size. The first three are the published price schedule and the fourth is a documented bound whose exact off-by-one is a reading of the wording — so the window is a dropdown, and the conclusion that survives moving it is that the cliff exists and sits at the window size, not that it sits at any particular integer. Everything else on the page is arithmetic.
