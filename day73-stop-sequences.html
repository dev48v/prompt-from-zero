PROMPTFROMZERO · DAY 73

✂️ Stop sequences — seventy-two days of this series changed what goes into the prompt. This one is about where the reply gets cut, and by whom: not the model, but the four lines of harness between the model and your parser. Nothing here simulates language ability — the generation is data, a record in one of five output formats with the producer's states enumerated, so every rate is an exact expectation over 448 records × 8 states and never a sample. The centrepiece needs no parameters: on JSON output the stop sequence } matches the reply's text 100.00% of the time and matches its tokens on 14.29% — and 100.00% of those token-level fires are early cuts inside the model's own data. It fires on exactly the replies it must not touch and misses every one it was installed for, decided by the type of the last field: "120.00"} ends in one token "}, and 120.00} ends in }. 🔴 Then the universal advice dies. "Set the stop to your format's closing token" delivers 0.00% on all three terminated formats, because the commonest return convention removes the matched string — which is precisely the character the parser was waiting for. Putting back the string the call already told you it matched costs zero tokens and is worth 85.71 points. 🔴 The second death is my own repair: you cannot fix token alignment by editing the stop string. 7 of the 9 repaired cells sit at 0.00% aligned, because a newline merges with whichever neighbour you put it beside. The edit that does win, wins for another reason entirely — }\n instead of } delivers 100.00% against 85.71%, one character, fewer tokens, and no reachable data collision, because prose never puts a newline directly after a brace. 🔴 A stop set is a minimum, not a vote: 12 of the 16 ways to fill four stop slots deliver 0.00%, the 16 subsets collapse to 8 behaviours, and a sequence containing another member can never fire at all — three sets measured at exactly 0.00%, plus a fourth zero my own containment test does not predict, where the format's own OPENING tag cuts the reply at character eight. 🔴 And two bugs cancel: the streaming loop every example ships shows the stop sequence to the user on 100.00% of calls, 9.2 characters of it, and thereby repairs the convention's damage — so the broken emitter parses 85.71% where the correct one parses 0.00%. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

Three things on this page are declared rather than derived, and each is labelled where it is used. The first is the tokenizer's merge list: it is hand-written, and its only claim is the one every real BPE vocabulary satisfies — that some entries glue punctuation to a neighbour. Which stop sequences misalign is therefore a property of a merge list rather than of any particular model; that some do is structural. The second is the producer's eight states — a greeting, three shapes of run-on, and nothing — whose probabilities are the two sliders above; the states themselves are enumerated, so a rate is a weighted sum and not a draw. The third is the return convention and the tie rule, which differ between APIs and are spelled out by none of them, so both settings of each are measured rather than assumed. Everything else — every match position, every token count, every parse — is a real string operation, so a 0.00% here means zero and not "none seen".

Every format's own closing token against every generation, matched two ways: on the decoded text, and only where it occupies whole tokens. early is a cut landing before the answer finished, which is the column that costs data rather than money.

WHAT THE CONTROL COSTS

Cutting client-side cannot mis-fire, and it pays for every token the model wrote after the answer. So the question is never which is safer — it is what the safety costs at each run-on rate.

ALL 16 WAYS TO FILL FOUR SLOTS

The API gives you four. Enumerate every subset of a realistic four: the cut is a minimum, so the ladder can only go down, and subsets sharing an antichain have to come back bit-identical.

Two stop sequences that end at the same index have to be resolved somehow, and no documentation says how. This panel asks what changing the rule actually moves.

THE DATA COLLISION, AND ITS CENSUS

IN-PAGE ASSERTIONS

The model does not decide where the reply ends

A stop sequence is the smallest feature in any inference API. It is a list of strings; when the generation produces one, generation halts. There is no model behaviour to argue about and nothing probabilistic in it, which is why this page can prove things rather than observe them. It is also, for exactly that reason, the part of a prompting stack where a defect is completely invisible: the call returns HTTP 200, the token count goes down, and the reply looks like a reply.

What is actually happening is a small pipeline with four independent decisions in it, and every one of them is a place where a correct implementation and a broken one produce output that looks the same. Where does the match get looked for — in the decoded text or in the token stream? What comes back — the text up to the match or through it? Which member of the set wins when two of them end in the same place? And what does a streaming emitter do with characters it has already sent by the time it realises they were the beginning of a stop sequence? Nothing in your prompt affects any of those four. They are the harness.

The parameter-free part: a stop sequence is text, and tokens are not

Take a reply ending in </answer> and ask a token-level matcher to find that string. It has to find it occupying whole tokens, because tokens are all it can see. When the reply ends right there, the final > is its own token and the match is clean. When the model then writes a newline — because it is about to add "Let me know if you need anything else" — the > and the newline merge into a single token, the last character of your stop sequence is now inside a token that also holds a character that is not yours, and the match is gone. The stop sequence did not change. The prompt did not change. A character after the stop sequence changed, and it decided whether the stop sequence exists.

The JSON version is worse, and worse in a way that inverts the feature. A compact object ends "120.00"}, and a quote followed by a brace is one of the most common merges in any vocabulary. So the brace is never on a boundary — unless the last value is a number, or there is a space before the brace, or the brace is inside a string, which is the one case where you did not want a match. On the traffic here the token-level matcher fires on 14.29% of replies and every single one of those fires is an early cut inside the model's own data. It misses 85.71% of the cuts it was installed to make and lands 100% of the ones that corrupt a record. A test suite with one clean example in it passes.

The law that makes most stop sets smaller than they look

The cut is the earliest end over the set. That one sentence has consequences nobody writes down. It means a set of stop sequences is a minimum and not a vote: adding one can only move the cut earlier, never later, so every addition is a risk and never a safeguard. It means that if one member occurs strictly inside another, the longer one can never fire — \n\n beside \n is dead, ### beside # is dead, ``` beside a backtick is dead. Not rare, not usually: dead, at exactly 0.00%, for every possible generation, and visible from the two strings without running anything.

And it means the containment test just described is incomplete, which is the interesting part. Put </answer> and > in the same set and containment says nothing is dead — the > sits at the very end of the longer string, so they tie rather than one preceding the other. Measure it and </answer> fires 0.00% of the time anyway, because the format's own opening tag <answer> carries a > at character eight. The reply is cut before it has said anything, for about four tokens a call. The structural test predicts three of the four zeros on that panel; the fourth comes from a string that is not in the stop set at all.

The advice every guide gives, priced

"Set your stop sequence to the closing token of your format." It is in every tutorial, and under the commonest return convention it delivers 0.00% — on tags, on JSON, on fenced blocks. The reason is one sentence of arithmetic: the convention hands back the text up to the match, the match is your closing token, and a keyed format with no closing token is exactly the thing Day 70 measured as unparseable. The feature deletes the one character that made the format safe, and it does it on 100% of successful calls. The saving grace is that the resulting failure is 100% loud, so you find out immediately, which is the only reason this is an embarrassing afternoon rather than a data-loss incident.

The fix is one line and costs nothing: the response tells you which stop sequence matched, so put it back. That is worth 85.71 points here, and in general it is worth exactly as much as the closing token is load-bearing in your format — which you can look up rather than guess.

The repair that does not work, and the one that does

If misalignment is the problem, pad the stop sequence so it lands on a boundary: put a newline in front of it. This page was built around that panel and the panel refutes it. Seven of the nine repaired cells are at 0.00% aligned, and the mechanism is symmetric — whichever end you extend, the character you add merges with the neighbour on that side. A newline in front of </answer> merges backwards with the > of the previous line's closing tag. You cannot align a stop sequence by editing the stop sequence, because alignment is not a property of the string.

What does win is on the same panel and wins for an unrelated reason. }\n in place of } delivers 100.00% against 85.71%, and not because it aligned — it is 0.00% aligned. It wins because the model's prose never puts a newline immediately after a brace, so the data collision that cost the plain brace its last 14.29 points is now unreachable. One character, no instruction tokens, fewer output tokens, and it changes which failures are possible rather than how often they happen. That is the difference between a mitigation and a fix, and it only surfaced because the panel measured delivery instead of measuring alignment.

Streaming, where two bugs cancel

A stop sequence has to be found in text the user is already watching arrive. The loop in every streaming example forwards each delta the moment it lands, so by the time the match completes the stop sequence is on the screen: 100.00% of calls, an average of 9.2 characters of </answer> visible in the chat. The correct emitter buffers, withholds any suffix that is a live prefix of a stop sequence, and flushes at end of stream. Its price is delay — up to 8 characters withheld, about half a character per token averaged over the stream — and it is a real price only for a long stop sequence, because a one-character stop can never be a proper prefix of itself and therefore holds nothing.

Two things about that pair are worth more than the pair. First, the broken emitter parses better: under the exclude convention it scores 85.71% where the correct emitter scores 0.00%, because leaking the stop sequence puts back exactly what the convention removed. Two defects, opposite signs, one clean-looking log. Second, the fix introduces a failure the leak did not have. Forget the final flush and you delete whatever is still held — 9.0 characters on 75.00% of calls with a </answer>\n stop, and those are precisely the calls where the model behaved perfectly and stopped on its own. The well-behaved reply is the one that breaks.

What to do with the null results

Two numbers here are worth reporting because they are zero. The tie rule — which of two stop sequences ending at the same index wins — moves the reported identity and the delivered text on 85.71% of calls, by exactly one character, and moves the verdict on 0.00% of them. Both spellings are already broken, so an undocumented rule turns out not to matter for the parse while mattering completely for any code that branches on which stop matched. And the sixteen subsets of four stop slots collapse to eight distinct behaviours, four of them bit-identical, because once \n is in the set nothing else in the set is ever consulted.

The honest summary of the page is the control row. Not using stop sequences at all delivers 85.71% and costs between 3.14% and 26.37% more output tokens depending on how talkative the model is. That is what the feature is worth. Five of the ten ways to configure it deliver less than that, none of the ten on the default format deliver more, and the one configuration that beats it — }\n on JSON, at 100.00% — differs from the standard advice by one character.

What this is not

This is not a claim about any specific API. The return convention and the tie rule differ between providers and are spelled out by neither, so both settings of each are on a dropdown and both are measured. It is not a claim about any specific tokenizer either: the merge list here is hand-written, and the only property it borrows from real vocabularies is that punctuation merges with its neighbours. Change the merge list and which stop sequences misalign changes; that some do is structural.

It is also not Day 70 and not Day 41. Day 70 chose the output format and parsed it, taking the whole reply as given. Day 41 delivered tokens as they arrived. Day 59 seeded the start of the reply and mentioned pairing a prefill with a stop sequence in one clause. This page is about the other end — the four decisions between the last token and your parser, none of which appear in a prompt and all of which change what your prompt was worth.

🔨 How it is built

Every number on the MEASURE tab comes from the block between ENGINE:BEGIN and ENGINE:END in this file's source. An independent Node verifier re-implements each load-bearing structure a second way — a regex tokenizer instead of a vocabulary walk, a brute-force index scan instead of indexOf, a character-by-character emitter instead of the closed-form one — and compares them set for set.

1. The generation is data, and its states are enumerated

There is no model. A generation is a record rendered in a format, optionally preceded by a greeting and optionally followed by one of three run-ons. Four customers × four amounts × four statuses × seven notes is 448 records, and two greetings × four continuations is 8 states, so one format's traffic is 3,584 strings and a rate is a weighted sum over all of them. The sliders move the weights, never a sample count, which is why a 0.00% on this page is a zero rather than a small number.

2. The tokenizer is the only declared component, and it is declared loudly

Greedy longest match over an explicit merge list, with every single character in the vocabulary so the tokenizer is total. The list holds entries like >\n, "}, \n\n and ``` — punctuation glued to a neighbour — because that is the property real vocabularies have and it is the only property the alignment results depend on. The page says which results are downstream of it.

3. The loop is closed form, and the lemma is checked rather than assumed

Running the harness character by character over every generation is quadratic and too slow to render. It is rewritten to find the match once in the whole string and then binary-search the token index that covers it, which is only legal because of a small lemma: the greedy choice at a position depends solely on the characters from that position forward, and the harness only ever appends whole tokens, so the tokenization of any prefix it can hold is exactly the prefix of the tokenization of the whole string. Boundaries therefore never move under truncation. The in-page assertions verify the lemma directly on every generation, and the verifier re-runs the whole harness the slow way and compares.

4. Two matchers, three emitters, two conventions, three tie rules

The matchers differ only in whether a hit must occupy whole tokens. The emitters are the delta-forwarding loop, the buffered one, and the buffered one with its flush removed. The hold amount is the longest suffix of what has been produced that is a proper prefix of some stop sequence, computed directly rather than through an automaton — so the verifier can build the automaton and check it against this.

5. Real parsers, and the three-way verdict

A tag scanner, a real JSON.parse, a fence stripper, a ### heading splitter and a line splitter. Each returns the fields it believes it read, or null when it can tell it failed, and the verdict is delivered / silently wrong / loudly failed — Day 70's taxonomy, deliberately, because the point here is that a different mechanism fills the same three columns.

6. The containment predicate is a prediction, and it is scored

deadIn looks only at the strings: a member is dead when another member occurs inside it ending before its end. It is printed beside the measured fire rate rather than instead of it, which is how that panel reports its own incompleteness — three of the four zeros are predicted and the fourth is not.

7. The bill is arithmetic, and the retry policy is stated

Output tokens billed per call are the tokens needed to cover the cut. A loud failure gets exactly one retry drawn from the same distribution; a silent one gets none. Tokens per correctly delivered record is the only column that compares a cheap wrong answer with an expensive right one, and it is where the control stops looking free.

8. The configuration space is enumerated, not argued

Four stop slots is sixteen subsets, so the whole space is enumerated and printed along with the behaviour class each subset lands in. Monotonicity — that adding a member cannot move the cut later — is asserted over every pair rather than stated as a property of minima.
