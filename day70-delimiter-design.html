PROMPTFROMZERO · DAY 70

🚧 Delimiter design — every guide says "use clear delimiters" and "wrap it in XML tags", and not one of them prices it or tells you what happens the day your data contains the delimiter. So this page builds the real encoder and the real decoder for 14 framing strategies — a real JSON.parse, a real RFC 4180 scanner, a real tag scanner, a real line scanner — and runs them over records whose values hold the characters business data actually holds. The first result needs no parameters at all: on a record with zero hazard characters in it, 5 of the 14 parsers hand you silently wrong data the moment the model writes "Sure! Here are the fields you asked for:", 8 of the 14 under at least one of six ordinary ways to wrap an answer, and exactly 3 survive all six — every one of them a format with an explicit closing token, which is not the same set as the formats with a key. 🔴 Then the free fixes win: deleting one .filter() from the newline parser is worth 30.15 points of delivered records for zero tokens, while telling the model to escape your delimiter costs 26 tokens to state and 0.31 to apply, recovers 2.80 points, and adds 0.69 points of silent corruption. Entity-escaping XML costs 22 tokens on every call forever and buys exactly 0.00. And comma-delimited output burns 73.28% of its token bill on retries that recover 0.000 records, because the collision is in the data and the retry reproduces it. Pure vanilla JavaScript, one file, no build step.

The two probe tables below are exact and parameter-free — they encode a fixed record, decode it, and print what came back, so no slider above changes a single cell in them. The corpus is random but the outcome is not sampled: for every record the whole finite set of producer states is decoded and weighted, which is why a rate like 0.00% on this page means zero and not "none seen". Only two numbers are declared rather than derived — how often a model wraps its answer in chatter, and how often it skips an escape — and both are on sliders, so you can set them to zero and watch which conclusions survive. (One of them is: at wrap = 0 the escaped pipe format is 99.54% correct.)

PROBE 1 · THE WRAPPING — exact, parameter-free, and zero hazard characters in the data

One record. Every value clean — not one comma, newline, pipe or quote anywhere in it. The only variable is what the model wrote around the answer.

PROBE 2 · ONE CHARACTER AT A TIME — also exact and parameter-free

Now the reverse: the model behaves perfectly and honours every escape, and a single ordinary character is dropped into one value, in every field position in turn. The worst outcome over the positions is shown.

The delimiter is not the safe part

      
Ask a model for five fields and you get back one string. Somewhere between the model's
        mouth and your database there is a parser, and that parser is the only thing standing between
        a correct answer and a row of garbage that looks exactly like a correct answer. The advice
        for this is universal and unpriced: use clear delimiters, wrap things in XML tags, ask for
        JSON. Nobody says what a tag costs per record, nobody says which characters break which
        scheme, and — this is the one that matters — nobody separates the two ways a parse can go
        wrong.

      
Those two ways are the whole subject. A loud failure throws, or returns the
        wrong number of fields, and you find out. A silent failure returns exactly
        k fields, every one a plausible string, and one of them is not the value the model
        sent. You never find out. Silent failures are not a smaller version of loud ones; they are
        the opposite kind of thing, because a loud failure costs you a retry and a silent failure
        costs you a wrong row forever. Every table on this page keeps them in separate columns, and
        every interesting result on this page is a result about the trade between them.


      
The first measurement needs no assumptions at all

      
Take one record and make every value boring. No commas. No newlines. No pipes, quotes,
        hashes, backticks. A perfectly clean, perfectly well-behaved record — the case the delimiter
        advice implicitly assumes. Encode it fourteen ways. Now let the model do the single most
        common thing a model does, which is not to lie or hallucinate but to be polite:
        Sure! Here are the fields you asked for:.

      
Five of the fourteen parsers return silently wrong data. Not an error — data.
        Comma-separated, pipe-separated, RFC 4180 quoted CSV, backslash-escaped pipes: all of them
        are single-line formats, so the greeting does not add a field, it gets absorbed into
        field one, and a sign-off gets absorbed into field k. The field count is right.
        The parse succeeds. Two of your five values are wrong and nothing anywhere says so. Allow the
        model the other five ordinary wrappings — a sign-off, a fence, a labelled fence — and it is
        eight of the fourteen, with only three surviving all six.

      
The newline format, by contrast, fails loudly on all five wrappings, and that is
        the good outcome. It fails loudly for a slightly humiliating reason: the newline is the
        delimiter that collides with the model's own boilerplate, so the chatter shows up as extra
        fields instead of extra characters, and a two-token count check sees it.
        Cheap safety here does not come from picking a delimiter your data lacks. It comes from
        picking a delimiter the noise has.


      
The keying argument is right about the wrong half

      
The standard defence of tags and headings is that they are keyed: the parser looks
        up customer rather than counting to position 1, so it cannot be
        knocked out of alignment. That is true, and the probe confirms it — prose in front of the
        answer is completely harmless to both ### customer headings and
        <customer> tags, while it destroys every positional format on
        the page.

      
But run the same probe with the prose behind the answer, and the two keyed formats
        separate. The tags are still fine. The headings are silently wrong. A
        ### key section has a start and no end, so it runs to the end of the
        string, and every "Let me know if you need anything else" the model appends lands
        inside your last field. The key check cannot see it: all the headings are present,
        all of them are known, the count is right.

      
So the thing doing the work in XML is not the key. It is the closing tag.
        Keying buys you immunity to whatever comes before your answer; termination buys you immunity
        to whatever comes after. Half the ceremony you are paying for buys half the protection you
        think you bought, and which half you got is decided by whether your framing has an explicit
        end. This is the failure mode I assumed was about exotic characters in the data, and it is
        not — it is about ordinary courtesy around the answer.

      
It holds at every field count, which is what makes it a statement about the syntax
        rather than about this corpus: sweep the schema from two fields to twelve and the closing tag
        survives a sign-off in all eleven cases, the heading is silently wrong in all eleven, the
        heading absorbs a greeting in all eleven, and the newline count check fails loudly in all
        eleven. Nothing about it is a rate.


      
The free fixes, which win

      
Three of the best interventions on this page cost nothing, and one of them is a deletion.
        The naive newline parser is split('\n').filter(x => x !== ''),
        and the filter is there because blank lines are ugly. Blank lines are also data: a
        blank comment field is a blank comment field, and dropping it slides every value after it up
        one position. Delete the filter and delivered records go from
        34.48% to 64.63% and silent corruption from 3.83% to
        0.00%, for zero extra tokens. Thirty points and a safety column, for
        removing eighteen characters from a parser.

      
Second: two lines that strip a code fence and clip to the outermost braces before calling
        JSON.parse. First-attempt success goes from 85.42% to
        92.85%, final delivery from 99.48% to 99.84%, and because the retries it
        avoids were real calls, the tokens per correct record fall from 108.2 to
        99.6. A free fix that makes the bill smaller is the best kind of result and
        it is available to anyone who has ever seen a model fence its JSON, which is everyone.

      
Third: the key check on ### headings — reject the response if a
        heading is unknown or missing — costs zero instruction tokens and zero frame tokens, and
        converts 2.00 of its 9.84 points of silent corruption into loud failures you can retry. It
        cannot touch the other 7.84, for the structural reason above.


      
The paid fixes, priced honestly

      
Now the escape conventions, which are what the guides mean by "handle the delimiter
        properly". Telling the model to escape a pipe as \| and a newline as
        \n is a real instruction that has to be written out, and it is
        written out on every single call for the life of the system. It costs
        26 tokens to state and 0.31 tokens to apply — eighty-odd
        times more expensive to explain than to perform. For that it recovers 2.80 points of
        delivered records.

      
It also adds 0.69 points of silent corruption, which I did not expect and which is
        the honest cost of the idea. An un-escaper is a new component, and it has a failure the
        plain format did not have: the day the model writes a Windows path raw, the un-escaper reads
        C:\temp and helpfully removes the backslash, producing
        C:temp — five characters of plausible garbage where a hard error
        would have been better. Adding a decoder adds a way to be wrong.

      
And here is the number that ends the argument. Set the wrapping slider to zero and the
        escaped pipe format is 99.54% correct. Every collision in the data is
        handled, completely, exactly as advertised. Turn the wrapping back to 8% and it is 91.58%,
        and all of the missing 8 points are the greeting. The escape convention solves 100%
        of the problem it was designed for and 0% of the problem you actually have.

      
Entity-escaping XML is the same story with the numbers taken to their limit: 22 extra
        instruction tokens on every call forever, and it buys exactly 0.00 points
        and 0.00 points of silent corruption, because raw XML was already at
        100.00%. The character probe shows it is not pointless — the escape closes a real
        hole, the value that literally contains </note>, which is
        silent corruption in raw XML. It is that no value in this corpus ever contains it. Whether
        you pay 22 tokens a call is therefore a question about your data, not a question about XML,
        and "escape everything defensively" is a decision with a price tag that nobody shows you.


      
The retry budget is two different products

      
Retries are the standard answer to parse failures, and how well they work has nothing to do
        with how many you allow. It depends entirely on where the failure lives. A retry is
        a fresh draw over the model's behaviour, so it can fix a fence and it cannot fix a comma:
        the comma is in your customer's name, the model will send it again, and it will fail
        identically for ever.

      
That falls straight out of the enumeration rather than being asserted. A record whose loud
        probability is exactly 1.0 has failed in every producer state, and for comma-delimited output
        that is 47.50% of records. So the retry ledger for CSV reads: 73.28% of the
        token bill spent on re-calls, 0.000 records recovered. Not "few". Zero, to
        three decimal places, by construction.

      
Against that, JSON: 100% of its failures are loud (that is what JSON.parse
        throwing means) and all of them come from the wrapper, so retries do exactly what retries are
        supposed to do — 85.42% to 100.00% across the budget, for 18% more tokens
        and 0.00% waste. Meanwhile the newline format spends its way from 59.49% to
        64.67%, a ceiling of five points, while the bill goes from 62 to 257 tokens per record and
        the waste share reaches 84.16%. Same knob, same setting, one of them buying everything and
        the other buying nothing.


      
The "wrap it in XML tags" advice, with the price on it

      
XML wins the correctness column outright, and it is not close: 100.00% delivered, 0.00%
        silent, no retries, unbothered by every wrapping and every character in the corpus. If
        correctness is the only column, stop reading and use tags.

      
The column it loses is the one that recurs forever. Its frame — the tags themselves, not the
        instruction — is 41.12 tokens per record, against 29.12 for JSON and 5.12
        for a newline. On a payload of 30.4 tokens of actual data, the tags cost more than the
        content. What that buys over JSON with the free fence-strip is the last
        0.16 of a point (100.00% against 99.84%) at +14.1% tokens
        per correct record, and the crossover search finds no hazard rate anywhere in
        [0, 1] at which the extra frame pays for itself — at a hazard rate of 1.00, with every
        single value in the corpus carrying a hazard character, JSON is still cheaper per correct
        record. There is a real reason to pay it, which is that 0.16 points of loss might be
        unacceptable in your domain, and that is a decision you should get to make with the number
        in front of you.

      
The instruction costs run the other way, and this is the part that surprised me most.
        JSON's instruction is the cheapest on the page — 32 tokens — while the escaped-pipe
        instruction is 59 and RFC 4180's is 61. You do not have to explain JSON's escaping rules
        because they are standard and the model already knows them. A bespoke delimiter convention
        is cheap in the frame and expensive in the explanation, and the explanation is the part you
        re-send on every call.


      
The dumbest thing on the page is also the most dangerous thing on the page

      
Two "helpful" repair heuristics are in the line-up because everybody writes them. Fold the
        extra fields into the last one when the count is too high. Keep the last k lines and
        assume the rest was preamble. Both eliminate loud failures completely — zero errors, zero
        retries, and the cheapest tokens per correct record on the page at two and three fields.

      
They achieve it by converting every loud failure into a silent one: 43.11%
        and 40.51% silent corruption respectively. On a scoreboard whose only
        columns are "errors" and "cost" — which is what most format comparisons actually print —
        keep-the-last-k is the best option available, by a distance, and it is quietly
        wrong about two records in five. That is the same shape as Day 68's count-check and Day 69's
        rule-trim: the single-column scoreboard does not just fail to rank the options, it inverts
        the ranking.

      
And the genuinely dumb baseline earns its place. Newline-separated with a
        length === k check has the cheapest frame on the page, needs no
        schema, no escaping and no explanation, and posts 0.00% silent
        corruption — matching XML and JSON, the two most expensive formats here, on the column that
        matters most. It loses 35.37% of records to hard failures, which is disqualifying at five
        fields. At two fields it costs 50 tokens per correct record
        against JSON's 52, and it never beats JSON again. "Just split on newlines" is not a silly
        answer; it is a correct answer whose validity expires at three fields, which is a much more
        useful thing to know.


      
What travels off this page

      
The census and the amplification law are the transferable part. Ten hazard characters
        counted in the corpus, and for each the per-field rate raised by
        1 − (1−p)k lands within 1.2 points of the measured
        per-record rate. That means you can do this arithmetic for your data before you pick
        a delimiter: grep your values for the character, divide, raise to the number of fields you
        ask for. The comma is in 11.93% of fields here and therefore 47.50% of five-field records,
        which is why comma-delimited output is not a format, it is an outage. The pipe is in 3.83% of
        fields and the bare # in 0.40%, which is why they feel safe: they
        are safe, in proportion to how rare they are in your text, and not one bit more.

      
Three things I did not expect before measuring. The escape convention was supposed to be
        the fix and it addresses a failure mode that contributes almost nothing once the model is
        allowed to be chatty. The keyed formats were supposed to be safe because they are keyed, and
        half of them are not, because keying is not termination. And the naive newline split was
        supposed to be the silent-corruption champion, and it turns out to have none — its
        failures are all loud, because a value with a newline in it always changes the line count.
        The parser that looks least careful is the one that never lies to you.


      
What this is not, and what it is not a repeat of

      
This is not a language-ability simulation and there is no model in it. Every number comes
        from running a real encoder into a real decoder and comparing strings. The two declared
        quantities — how often a model wraps its answer, how often it skips an escape — are dials,
        not findings, and every conclusion above is either parameter-free or stated together with
        what happens when you turn the dial to zero.

      
Token counts are estimates. The tokenizer here is a deterministic stand-in, not a real BPE
        vocabulary, so treat the absolute numbers as approximate and the ratios as the claim — and
        note that the frame, which is what the ratios are about, is made of exactly the punctuation
        runs the merge table keeps whole, which is the part it gets right.

      
Day 66 was also about delimiters and is the opposite direction:
        spotlighting fences off untrusted input so the model treats it as data, and its
        failure is the model obeying something it should not. Here the delimiters are on the
        output and the failure is a parser, not a model. Day 16 is about
        deciding what shape to ask for; this is about what happens to that shape on the way home.
        Day 68 asks which answer belongs to which question across a batch; this asks
        which substring belongs to which field inside one answer. Day 59 locks the
        format by seeding the response instead of describing it, which is a different lever on the
        same problem and would remove most of the wrapping failures above — at the cost of a
        mechanism current models no longer accept.

    

  






  

    

      
🔨 How it is built


      
1. Fourteen real codecs, not fourteen descriptions

      
Every strategy is an enc / dec pair, and
        the decoders are real: JSON.parse in a try/catch, a hand-written
        RFC 4180 reader with quote-doubling and an unterminated-quote error, a line scanner for
        ### key sections, a non-greedy tag matcher per key, and the various
        split-and-count parsers people actually ship. A verdict is decided
        by string comparison against the record that went in, never by inspection of the text.


      
2. Three verdicts, because two is the mistake

      
ok means every field came back identical.
        loud means the decoder refused. silent
        means the decoder returned k confident fields and at least one differs. Collapsing
        the last two into "failure" is what makes fold-the-extras look like a good idea.


      
3. The producer is enumerated, not sampled

      
A model does one of three things to a correct answer — nothing, prose around it, or a fence
        around it — and independently either honours a requested escape or does not. That is at most
        six states with known probabilities, so every state is encoded, decoded and weighted, and the
        per-record outcome is an exact expectation. There is no Monte-Carlo noise anywhere in the
        outcome columns; the only randomness on the page is which values the corpus drew.


      
4. Retries fall out of the same enumeration

      
With per-attempt loud probability L, the expected attempts under a budget of
        r retries is Σj=0..r Lj, delivered-correct is
        O times that, and the unrecoverable share is Lr+1. The important
        case needs no special handling: a data collision fails in every state, so
        L = 1 exactly, the sum is r+1, and the record is lost having been
        paid for r+1 times. Those records are counted separately as "deterministic", which
        is where the wasted-token column comes from.


      
5. Two probes that no slider can touch

      
The wrapping probe builds a record from the first clean value of every field, so it contains
        no hazard character at all, and applies six fixed wrappings. The character probe holds the
        producer perfect and drops one payload — a comma, a newline, ### note,
        </note>, a blank string — into one value at a time, in every
        field position. Both are pure functions of the codecs. They are the reason the headline result
        does not depend on any assumption about model behaviour.


      
6. Instruction cost and frame cost, separated

      
Every codec carries the English sentence that would actually have to be sent, and that is
        tokenised as the per-call instruction cost. The frame cost is measured by difference: encode
        the record, tokenise it, and subtract the tokens of the bare values. So the tags, braces,
        quotes and separators are isolated from the payload, which is what makes "41.12 tokens of
        frame on 30.4 tokens of data" a statement rather than an impression.


      
7. The tokenizer, and the identity that keeps the page live

      
Letters at about four characters per token, digits at three, a single space riding free on
        the following word, everything else one token each — except a small table of punctuation runs
        a real vocabulary keeps whole (```, ###,
        {", ":, </,
        &lt;). It is indexed by first character so the scan only tries a
        merge where one could start.

      
Every wrapper begins and ends at a newline, and no merge in that table and no letter or digit
        run can cross a newline, so the token count of a wrapped answer is exactly the count of the
        answer plus a constant (24 for the prose, 4 for the fence). Using that identity instead of
        re-tokenising six full strings per record is what makes the sliders responsive — and it is
        asserted, not assumed: the tests re-tokenise every wrapped text and compare.


      
8. What the assertions pin down

      
    
        
  • the RNG: determinism, seed sensitivity, uniformity to under 5% per decile;
    • 
        
  • every escape/un-escape pair is a round trip on the whole corpus, and the RFC 4180 reader
          is checked against hand-built rows including doubled quotes and an unterminated quote;
    • 
        
  • every codec is lossless on clean data — encode, decode, compare, for every
          record with no hazard character in it — which is the floor a codec has to clear before any
          of its other numbers mean anything;
    • 
        
  • the three outcome probabilities sum to 1 for every record and every codec;
    • 
        
  • a record with loud probability 1 gains exactly nothing from any retry budget;
    • 
        
  • the wrap-additivity identity, re-tokenised;
    • 
        
  • both probe matrices, cell by cell, against the values the prose quotes — and the
          closing-tag result re-run at every field count from 2 to 12;
    • 
        
  • and the numbers in the headline, recomputed from the engine so the prose cannot drift.
    • 
      


      
9. Three things that were not what I expected

      
The escape convention was the wrong fix. I built it as the answer to
        delimiter collision, and it is — at wrap = 0 it delivers 99.54%. Then the
        model says hello and it delivers 91.58%, and nothing about the escape addresses the
        difference. Measuring the mechanism you designed for is not the same as measuring the one
        that fires.

      
Keying is not termination. I had ### key in the
        line-up as a cheap stand-in for XML on the theory that a key is a key. Prose in front:
        identical. Prose behind: silently wrong, invisibly, with every key present and correct. The
        closing tag is the load-bearing half of the ceremony.

      
Adding a decoder added a failure. Escaped pipes have more silent
        corruption than raw pipes (8.41% against 7.72%), because an un-escaper eats a real backslash
        in a Windows path. Every layer you add to be safer is also a layer that can be wrong.


      
10. What this is not

      
No model, no language ability, no benchmark. The corpus is generated from twelve hand-written
        field templates and the hazard rates are properties of that generator, so the headline
        percentages describe this corpus and not your data — which is precisely why the census and the
        amplification law are on the page, since those you can recompute for your own values in an
        afternoon. The two behavioural rates are declared and adjustable. Absolute token counts are
        estimates; the frame ratios are the claim.
